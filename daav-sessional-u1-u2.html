1.1 Big Data: Introduction and evolution

Big data is data too big, too fast or too messy for one ordinary database server to store and analyse in reasonable time. (itna data ki ek normal DB machine haath khade kar de)

Definition: Big data refers to data sets whose volume, velocity and variety exceed the capacity of traditional database systems to capture, store, manage and analyse them, and which therefore need new, distributed and cost-effective forms of processing to extract insight and support decisions.
  • Evolution: file systems → relational databases (Codd's relational model, 1970) → data warehouses for reporting → web-scale data from search engines and social media → Google's GFS (2003) and MapReduce (2004) papers → open-source Hadoop (2006) → NoSQL, Spark and cloud platforms.
  • The term "3 Vs" (volume, velocity, variety) is usually traced to Doug Laney's 2001 META Group note; later authors added more Vs.
  • Why traditional RDBMS fails at this scale:
    • Scale-up only: an RDBMS mostly grows by buying a bigger server; cost rises steeply and there is a hardware ceiling. (badi machine ki bhi limit hai)
    • Fixed schema (schema-on-write): table structure must be designed before loading; JSON, logs, images and video do not fit rows and columns.
    • Speed: continuous streams (clicks, sensor readings) arrive faster than row-by-row inserts and joins can keep up.
    • Cost: licensed database software plus high-end storage for petabytes is expensive.
    • Single point of failure: one large server failing stops everything.
  • Big data approach: split data across many cheap (commodity) machines, move the computation to where data sits, store raw data first and decide structure later (schema-on-read).

Big Data driving factors

  • Everything went online: the web, e-commerce and now smart/IoT objects mean almost every action (a click, a swipe, a sensor reading) leaves a digital trace. (har cheez ab online, har cheez data chhod rahi hai)
  • More kinds of sources add data every year: social media, sensor networks, digital images/video, cell phones, purchase/transaction records, web logs, medical records, archives, military/surveillance systems, e-commerce, and large scientific research projects.
  • Early-2010s industry studies (e.g. IDC's "Digital Universe" reports) projected global data volume to grow many-fold through 2020 — figures like "tens of zettabytes" and "hundreds of times growth since 2005" are widely quoted in big-data tutorials. Treat these as order-of-magnitude illustrations of Volume, not numbers to reproduce precisely: different reports quote different exact figures, and none of them are current any more.

Advantages of Big Data

  • Better decision-making: more usable data means business decisions based on evidence rather than guesswork.
  • Reduced cost of business processes: distributed storage/processing on commodity hardware plus open-source tools cut infrastructure spend versus scaling up one large server.
  • Fraud detection: models trained on transaction history flag anomalies (an impossible travel pattern on a card, an odd purchase time) faster than manual review.
  • Increased productivity: analysts and data scientists get a quick, tool-assisted overview of large data instead of sifting through it by hand.
  • Improved customer service: data from social media, email and CRM systems exposes customer pain points, enabling targeted, personalised offers.
  • Increased agility: insight ahead of competitors lets a business adjust products, communication and strategy faster.

Disadvantages / challenges of Big Data

  • Lack of talent: skilled big-data engineers and data scientists are scarce, so hiring is slow and expensive, especially for smaller companies.
  • Security risks: large pooled data sets attract attackers; a breach can leak sensitive information and damage trust.
  • Compliance: personal/confidential data must be stored, handled and processed to government and industry rules, which gets harder to manage as volume grows.
Mnemonic Advantages D-C-F-P-S-A: Decisions, Cost, Fraud, Productivity, Service, Agility. Disadvantages T-S-C: Talent, Security, Compliance.
Quick recall Big data = data beyond traditional DB capacity (volume/velocity/variety). RDBMS fails due to scale-up limits, fixed schema, speed and cost.

1.2 Characteristics of Big Data (the Vs)

Big data is described by its "Vs": started as 3 Vs, most textbooks now teach 5 Vs.

Definition: The characteristics of big data are the properties that distinguish it from traditional data, commonly summarised as the 5 Vs: Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity and Value.
  • Volume: the sheer amount of data, measured in terabytes (TB), petabytes (PB), exabytes (EB). Example: years of transaction records of a bank. (kitna zyada)
  • Velocity: the speed at which data is generated and must be processed, from batch (daily) to near real-time (every second). Example: UPI payments being checked for fraud as they happen. (kitni tezi se)
  • Variety: different forms of data: structured tables, semi-structured JSON/XML, unstructured text, images, audio, video. (kitne prakar ka)
  • Veracity: trustworthiness and quality: noise, missing values, duplicates, fake reviews, sensor errors. (kitna sach)
  • Value: the business or social benefit extracted; data that gives no insight is only a storage cost. (kitne kaam ka)
  • Value is often judged by whether a big-data initiative shows a positive Return on Investment (ROI) — insight that does not improve decisions or revenue does not justify the storage/processing cost.
  • Extensions some books add: Variability (meaning or flow rate of data changes over time, e.g. seasonal sale spikes, a word changing sense) and Visualization (presenting huge data so humans can understand it). Some texts also list Validity or Volatility.

1.3 Types of Big Data

Data comes in three shapes: neat tables, tagged text, and a free-form pile. (table, tag wala, aur bikhra hua)

Definition: Based on organisation, big data is classified as structured (fixed schema, rows and columns), semi-structured (self-describing tags or keys but no rigid schema) and unstructured (no predefined data model).
  • Structured: predefined schema, stored in rows/columns, easy to query with SQL. Examples: bank account table, student marks sheet, IRCTC booking records.
  • Semi-structured: no fixed table schema, but tags/keys mark the fields, so it is self-describing. Examples: JSON from an API, XML files, email (header fields + free body), web server logs.
  • Unstructured: no data model; needs special processing (text mining, image/speech analysis). Examples: WhatsApp messages, YouTube videos, CCTV footage, PDFs, call recordings.
  • Many books note that unstructured data forms the largest share of today's data; the exact percentage quoted varies by source.
  • Some sources put this as high as ~80% of an organisation's data being unstructured — a widely repeated estimate useful for a sense of scale, not a number to cite as an exact measurement.

1.4 Sources of Big Data

Data is produced by people, by machines, and by the business systems of organisations.

Definition: Sources of big data are the origins from which large data sets are generated; they are broadly grouped as human-generated, machine-generated and organisation (transactional) data.
  • Human-generated (social data): posts, likes, comments, tweets, photos, videos, emails, blogs, product reviews, search queries. Mostly unstructured.
  • Machine-generated (sensor data): IoT devices, smart meters, CCTV, GPS in cabs, satellite imagery, RFID tags, and logs written by web servers and apps (clickstreams). High velocity.
  • Modern connected consumer electronics — smart TVs, wearables, connected cars — are further examples of machine-generated sources, alongside IoT sensors, CCTV and GPS.
  • Organisational / transactional: sales, billing, banking and UPI transactions, stock trades, ERP (inventory, HR) and CRM (customer) records. Mostly structured.
  • Other named sources in books: public/open data (government portals such as data.gov.in, census), scientific data (genomics, telescopes), healthcare records, mobile call detail records (CDRs).
  • The notes also name archives and military/surveillance data among machine/organisational sources, and public records alongside open government data — all still fit the Human / Machine / Organisation grouping above.
  • Also split as internal (inside the company: ERP, logs) vs external (outside: social media, open data, purchased data).

1.5 Examples and applications of Big Data

Anywhere lots of data meets a decision, big data analytics is used.

Definition: Big data applications are uses of large-scale data collection and analysis to improve decisions, predict outcomes or automate processes in a domain such as retail, banking, healthcare or transport.
  • Retail / e-commerce: "customers who bought this also bought" recommendations from purchase and browsing history.
  • Banking and payments: flag unusual transactions (location, amount, time) in near real time.
  • Healthcare: analyse electronic health records and medical images; track outbreaks.
  • Transport and delivery: GPS data from vehicles to estimate arrival times and plan routes (cab and food-delivery apps).
  • Media and entertainment: streaming services suggest shows from viewing history.
  • Social media: sentiment analysis of posts about a brand, film or election.
  • Manufacturing / IoT: predictive maintenance from machine sensor readings.
  • Weather and science: forecasting from satellite and sensor data.
Example Fraud check on a card payment:
Input: card used in Delhi at 10:00, then in another country at 10:05
Rule / model compares with past behaviour (Velocity + Volume of history)
Physically impossible travel → transaction held, SMS sent to customer
Decision in seconds from streaming + historical data

Applications by sector (a closer look)

  • Education: customised/dynamic learning programs built from each student's own learning history, course material reframed from real-time monitoring, improved grading systems, and career prediction from a student's full academic record — e.g. the notes cite the University of Alabama, a large public university, using analytics on student data to guide recruitment and retention instead of relying on manual review.
  • Healthcare: lower treatment cost by cutting unnecessary diagnosis, predicting outbreaks of epidemics, catching preventable diseases earlier, and evidence-based medicine drawn from past case outcomes — e.g. a wearable such as the Apple Watch can feed real-time health data into a patient's electronic health record.
  • Government: welfare schemes targeted through faster, more informed policy decisions, identifying areas needing urgent attention, tracking agriculture/land/livestock records, and tackling national challenges such as unemployment, terrorism and energy-resource exploration; also cyber-security — fraud/deceit recognition and catching tax evaders — e.g. the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) analyses data to look for patterns behind food-borne illness outbreaks.
  • Media and entertainment: predicting what audiences want, on-demand content scheduling, insight mined from customer reviews, and effective ad targeting — e.g. Spotify builds its music recommendations from each listener's own play history across its user base.
  • Weather: sensor and satellite data feed weather forecasting, the study of global warming, understanding natural-disaster patterns, preparing for crises, and predicting usable-water availability.
  • Transportation: route planning, real-time congestion/traffic control, and flagging accident-prone stretches to improve safety — e.g. Uber analyses driver, vehicle and trip data to predict demand, position drivers and set fares.
  • Banking: anti-money-laundering and fraud-analytics software (e.g. SAS AML) scans transactions for suspicious patterns for banks and credit unions.
  • Marketing: the shift from surveys and mass advertising to digital marketing, where purchase and browsing data drives targeted campaigns — e.g. Amazon studies purchase and payment patterns across its customers to design offers.
  • Business insights: a large share of the data organisations collect is unstructured or never analysed (often quoted as roughly 60%, varies by source); mining it supports decisions on profit, customer satisfaction and product development — e.g. Netflix studies viewing behaviour to decide what to recommend or commission.
  • Space: agencies mine data from satellites, probes and rovers to simulate flight paths and mission conditions before a launch — e.g. NASA uses data from Mars rovers and orbiters this way.
Quick recall Retail (recommendations), banking (fraud), healthcare (records), transport (routes/ETA), telecom (churn), government (census, smart city) · plus education, media, weather, marketing, business insights, space.

1.6 Big Data technologies

No single tool does it all; big data systems are a stack of layers, each with its own tools.

Definition: Big data technologies are the software frameworks and platforms used to ingest, store, process, analyse and visualise very large and diverse data sets, typically on distributed clusters.
  • Ingestion: bring data in. Sqoop (bulk RDBMS ↔ Hadoop), Flume (log collection), Kafka (event streaming).
  • Storage: HDFS (distributed file system), cloud object storage (e.g. Amazon S3), data lakes.
  • NoSQL databases: flexible-schema, scale-out stores: HBase, Cassandra, MongoDB, Redis, Neo4j.
  • Batch processing: process stored data in large chunks: Hadoop MapReduce, Apache Spark.
  • Streaming (real-time): process data as it arrives: Kafka Streams, Spark Streaming, Apache Flink, Apache Storm.
  • Analytics: SQL-on-Hadoop (Hive, Impala), scripting (Pig), machine learning (Spark MLlib), languages R and Python.
  • Visualisation: Tableau, Power BI, Python/R plotting libraries.
  • Cloud platforms package these as managed services (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud).
  • Implementation languages: Hadoop's core is written in Java; MapReduce jobs are most naturally written in Java, but Hadoop Streaming lets a mapper/reducer be written in Python (or any language that reads stdin/writes stdout), and Hadoop Pipes gives a native C++ API — R is used mainly at the analytics/ML layer, not to write the core framework.

1.7 Integrating diverse data

Data from many systems in many formats must be combined into one consistent view before it can be analysed. (alag-alag jagah ka data ek jagah, ek format mein)

Definition: Data integration is the process of combining data from heterogeneous sources (different formats, schemas and systems) into a unified, consistent and usable view for analysis.
  • Challenges of integrating diverse data:
    • Heterogeneous formats: CSV, JSON, XML, tables, images.
    • Schema mismatch: cust_id in one system, customerNo in another; different units (INR vs USD, dd-mm vs mm-dd dates).
    • Data quality (veracity): duplicates, missing and conflicting values.
    • Volume and velocity: batch and streaming data must be combined.
    • Semantic conflicts: same word, different meaning ("customer" = buyer vs account holder).
    • Security and privacy when data leaves its original system.
  • Schema mapping: define how each source field corresponds to a field in the target schema (e.g. cust_id → customer_id), including type and unit conversions.
  • Entity resolution (record linkage): decide that "R. Sharma, Pune" and "Rahul Sharma, Pune" are the same customer.
  • ETL (Extract, Transform, Load): transform on a staging server before loading; suits data warehouses with a fixed schema.
  • ELT (Extract, Load, Transform): load raw data first, transform inside the powerful target (Hadoop, cloud warehouse); suits big data.
  • Other approaches: data virtualisation / federation (query sources in place without copying), change data capture (move only changed rows), streaming integration with Kafka.

1.8 Introduction to Hadoop

Hadoop lets a cluster of cheap computers store and process huge data together, as if it were one big machine. (bahut saari sasti machines milkar ek badi machine)

Definition: Apache Hadoop is an open-source Java-based framework for distributed storage and distributed processing of very large data sets across clusters of commodity hardware, using simple programming models, and designed to detect and handle failures at the software level.
  • History: created by Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella out of the Nutch web-search project, based on Google's GFS (2003) and MapReduce (2004) papers; became a separate project in 2006 and an Apache top-level project in 2008. Named after a toy elephant belonging to Cutting's son.
  • Core modules: Hadoop Common (libraries), HDFS (storage), YARN (resources, added in Hadoop 2), MapReduce (processing model).
  • Key idea: data locality: send the program to the nodes where the data blocks already are, instead of moving terabytes over the network. (data ke paas code bhejo)
  • Master–slave design: a master keeps metadata and schedules work, many slave (worker) nodes store data and run tasks.
  • Real-world adoption: well-known users include Facebook, Yahoo, Netflix and eBay; vendors and integrators such as Microsoft, IBM, Intel, MapR, Cloudera and Hortonworks built products and distributions around Hadoop.

Features / advantages

  • Scalable: add nodes to grow storage and compute (scale-out).
  • Fault tolerant: blocks are replicated (default 3 copies); failed tasks are re-run on another node.
  • Cost-effective: runs on commodity hardware; open source (Apache License 2.0).
  • Flexible: stores any data type without a schema up front.
  • High throughput: parallel processing of large batches.

Limitations

  • Small files problem: every file and block is metadata in NameNode memory, so millions of tiny files overload it.
  • Batch-oriented: MapReduce is not suited to low-latency, interactive or real-time queries.
  • Slow for iterative jobs (machine learning, graph algorithms) because MapReduce writes intermediate data to disk; Spark addresses this with in-memory processing.
  • Complex to program and manage: low-level Java MapReduce code; many moving parts.
  • Security was minimal by default in early versions (Kerberos integration added later).
  • In Hadoop 1.x the NameNode was a single point of failure; Hadoop 2 added NameNode High Availability.

Uses of Hadoop

  • Analytics and big data: research, production data processing and analytics that need to process terabytes to petabytes of data.
  • Data storage and archiving: low-cost storage on commodity hardware for transactions, clickstreams, sensor/machine data.
  • Data lakes: complements a data lake by storing large amounts of raw, unrefined data without preprocessing.
  • Marketing analytics: storing and analysing customer relationship management (CRM) data.
  • Risk management: banks, insurers and other financial firms build risk-analysis and risk-management models on Hadoop.
  • AI and machine learning: processes data and runs model-training workloads for machine learning applications.

1.9 HDFS: Hadoop Distributed File System

HDFS cuts a huge file into big blocks and keeps 3 copies of each block on different machines.

Definition: HDFS is Hadoop's distributed file system that stores very large files as fixed-size blocks across many DataNodes, replicating each block for fault tolerance, and is designed for write-once, read-many, high-throughput streaming access.
  • NameNode (master): stores the metadata: namespace (directory tree), file → block list, block → DataNode locations, permissions. It does not store the actual data. (register rakhta hai, maal nahi)
  • DataNode (slave): stores the actual blocks on local disk, serves read/write requests, and sends a heartbeat (every 3 s by default) and periodic block reports to the NameNode. A cluster may have anywhere from a handful of DataNodes up to 500 or more; more DataNodes means more storage and more parallel I/O.
  • Secondary NameNode: periodically merges the edit log into the fsimage (checkpointing) so the NameNode restarts faster. It is not a hot standby. In HA setups a Standby NameNode does this job.
  • Block: a file is split into fixed-size blocks; default 128 MB in Hadoop 2.x/3.x (64 MB in 1.x), configurable via dfs.blocksize. The last block holds only the remaining bytes, so it can be smaller.
  • Replication factor: default 3 (dfs.replication). Rack-aware default placement: 1st replica on the writer's node (or a random node), 2nd on a node in a different rack, 3rd on a different node in the same rack as the 2nd. A rack is simply the physical group of nodes (commonly on the order of 30–40 machines) sharing one network switch; choosing the closest rack for a read/write cuts network traffic.
  • If a DataNode stops sending heartbeats, the NameNode marks it dead and re-replicates its blocks from surviving copies.
  • Write-once, read-many: files can be appended but not edited in the middle; good for large sequential reads, poor for random updates.
  • Hadoop 3 adds optional erasure coding, which gives fault tolerance with less storage overhead than 3× replication.
  • fsimage and edit log: the NameNode keeps its metadata in two files. The fsimage is a complete snapshot of the namespace at one point in time; the edit log is an append-only record of every change (create, rename, append, ...) since that snapshot. On restart the NameNode reloads the fsimage and replays the edit log to rebuild its current state, instead of writing a brand-new snapshot on every single change.
  • The Secondary NameNode periodically pulls the edit log over RPC and merges it into its own copy of the fsimage (this is exactly what "checkpointing" means), so a restarted NameNode has a shorter edit log to replay and recovers faster — but, as above, it still cannot take over automatically the way a Standby NameNode in HA mode can.
  • Because the NameNode keeps this state in memory, production clusters typically also persist it to two local disks (in case one disk fails) and to an NFS-mounted volume (in case the whole machine fails).
  • Terms: Heartbeat — the signal a DataNode sends the NameNode at regular intervals; missing heartbeats mark it dead. Balancing — after a DataNode dies, its blocks become under-replicated, so the NameNode signals DataNodes holding surviving copies of those blocks to replicate them elsewhere, restoring even distribution. Replication — the copying itself is carried out by the DataNodes, not the NameNode. Rule: no two replicas of the same block are ever placed on the same DataNode.
Example A 500 MB file, block size 128 MB, replication factor 3:
Number of blocks = ceil(500 / 128) = ceil(3.906...) = 4
Block sizes = 128 + 128 + 128 + (500 − 3 × 128) = 128, 128, 128, 116 MB
Block replicas stored = 4 × 3 = 12
Raw disk used = 500 × 3 = 1500 MB (the last block uses only 116 MB per copy, not 128)
4 blocks, 12 replicas, 1500 MB raw storage

Features of HDFS

  • Distributed data storage across many DataNodes rather than one machine.
  • Blocks reduce seek time: splitting a huge file into fixed blocks makes parallel reads/writes far faster than seeking through one giant file on a single disk.
  • High availability: the same block sits on multiple DataNodes, so data stays reachable even if one copy is unreachable.
  • High reliability: even if several DataNodes go down at once, work can continue as long as one replica of each block survives.
  • High fault tolerance: lost blocks are automatically re-replicated from surviving copies.

Limitations of HDFS

  • Small file problem: lots of small files mean lots of seeks between DataNodes to fetch each one, which is a very inefficient access pattern (and, as covered under Hadoop's limitations, it also bloats NameNode memory since every file/block is metadata there).

1.10 MapReduce

MapReduce breaks one big job into many small parallel jobs (Map), then combines their answers (Reduce). (kaam baanto, phir jodo)

Definition: MapReduce is a programming model and processing engine for parallel, distributed batch processing of large data sets, in which a map function converts input records into intermediate key–value pairs and a reduce function aggregates all values that share the same key.
  • Input split: logical chunk of input (usually one HDFS block); a RecordReader turns it into (key, value) records, e.g. (byte offset, line). Its job is purely to hand the Map function well-formed records — the key here is positional information, the value is the actual data.
  • Map phase: user's map function runs on each record in parallel on many nodes, producing intermediate (key, value) pairs. Map output is written to the node's local disk, not to HDFS. A map function may emit zero, one or many pairs per input record.
  • Combiner (optional): a "mini-reducer" on the map side that pre-aggregates to cut network transfer.
  • Partitioner: decides which reducer gets each key (default: hash of key mod number of reducers), commonly written as partition = key.hashCode() % numReduceTasks.
  • Shuffle and sort: framework transfers map outputs to reducers and sorts/groups them so each key arrives with a list of all its values. Shuffling can start as soon as some map tasks finish — it does not wait for every mapper to complete, which is part of why the pipeline overlaps rather than running fully sequentially.
  • Reduce phase: user's reduce function processes (key, list of values) and writes the final output to HDFS.
  • OutputFormat: once the reducer is done, a RecordWriter writes each final (key, value) pair to the output file, one record per line, key and value separated by whitespace.
  • Formally: map: (k1, v1) → list(k2, v2), reduce: (k2, list(v2)) → list(k3, v3).

1.11 YARN and Hadoop architecture

YARN is Hadoop's "operating system": it hands out CPU and memory on the cluster to whichever application needs them.

Definition: YARN (Yet Another Resource Negotiator), introduced in Hadoop 2, is the cluster resource-management layer that separates resource management and job scheduling from data processing, allowing multiple processing engines (MapReduce, Spark, etc.) to share one Hadoop cluster.
  • ResourceManager (RM): master daemon, one per cluster; arbitrates all cluster resources. Two parts: Scheduler (allocates resources, e.g. FIFO, Capacity, Fair schedulers) and ApplicationsManager (accepts job submissions, starts the ApplicationMaster, restarts it on failure). The scheduler side also tracks each job's priority, its dependencies on other jobs, and timing information.
  • NodeManager (NM): slave daemon on every worker node; launches and monitors containers and reports the node's resource usage and health to the RM.
  • ApplicationMaster (AM): one per application; negotiates containers from the RM and works with NMs to run and monitor the application's tasks.
  • Container: a bundle of resources (memory, CPU cores) on one node in which a task runs.
  • Job flow: client submits → RM starts AM in a container → AM requests containers → RM grants → AM asks NMs to launch tasks → tasks report to AM → AM finishes and deregisters.
  • YARN replaced Hadoop 1's JobTracker/TaskTracker, which mixed resource management with MapReduce and limited scalability.
  • Storage layer (HDFS) and compute layer (YARN) run side by side on the same worker nodes, which is what makes data locality possible.

Features of YARN

  • Multi-tenancy: several processing engines (MapReduce, Spark, Hive, ...) and several applications/users can share the same cluster at the same time.
  • Scalability: moving resource management out of MapReduce lets a YARN cluster scale far beyond what Hadoop 1's JobTracker could manage.
  • Cluster utilisation: the ResourceManager hands out CPU and memory dynamically to whichever application needs them, instead of Hadoop 1's fixed map/reduce "slots" which often sat idle.
  • Compatibility: existing Hadoop 1 MapReduce applications keep running on YARN unchanged, since MapReduce is now just one of several application types on top of it.

1.13 Introduction to NoSQL

NoSQL databases drop the fixed-table model so they can spread data across many servers and store flexible data. (table ka bandhan nahi, machines badhao aur scale karo)

Definition: NoSQL ("Not only SQL") databases are non-relational, usually schema-less, distributed databases designed to store and retrieve large volumes of structured, semi-structured and unstructured data with horizontal scalability and high availability.

Why NoSQL

  • Scale-out: RDBMS clusters are hard to shard; NoSQL is built to run on clusters of commodity machines.
  • Flexible schema: new fields can be added without ALTER TABLE; suits fast-changing apps and semi-structured data.
  • Impedance mismatch: application objects (nested lists, maps) do not map neatly to flat relational rows; NoSQL stores them closer to their in-memory shape.
  • High availability through replication across nodes and data centres.
  • Big data volume and velocity from web, mobile and IoT applications.

Features

  • Non-relational: no joins-based table model (most types).
  • Schema-less / schema-on-read, sometimes called dynamic schema: fields can be reshaped to meet a new requirement without a migration step.
  • Distributed: sharding (split data across nodes) and replication (copy data across nodes).
  • BASE consistency model, often eventual consistency, instead of strict ACID (varies by product).
  • Usually open source, simple APIs, commodity hardware.
  • Global availability: data replicated across servers, data centres and clouds so users anywhere reach a nearby copy — a different benefit from plain high availability, which is about surviving a node failure rather than cutting latency for distant users.

1.14 Types of NoSQL databases

Four families: a dictionary, a folder of JSON files, a wide sparse table, and a network of dots and lines.

Definition: NoSQL databases are classified by data model into four types: key-value stores, document stores, column-family (wide-column) stores and graph databases.
  • Key-value store: simplest; a giant hash table mapping a unique key to a value the database does not look inside. Very fast get/put by key. Uses: session data, shopping carts, caching, user preferences, leaderboards. Examples: Redis, Memcached, Riak, Amazon DynamoDB.
  • Document store: value is a self-describing document (JSON, BSON, XML); the database understands its fields, so you can query and index inside it. Uses: product catalogues, content management, user profiles. Examples: MongoDB, CouchDB, Cloudant.
  • Column-family (wide-column) store: rows identified by a row key, columns grouped into column families; rows can have different columns (sparse). Good for very large, write-heavy data. Uses: time-series, event logging, messaging, IoT data. Examples: Apache Cassandra, Apache HBase (both inspired by Google Bigtable).
  • Graph database: stores entities as nodes and relationships as edges, both with properties; traversing relationships is fast. Uses: social networks, recommendation, fraud rings, route finding. Examples: Neo4j, Amazon Neptune, ArangoDB.

1.15 Aggregate data models

Instead of splitting an order across many tables, store the whole order (with its items and address) as one unit. (poora packet ek saath rakho)

Definition: An aggregate is a collection of related objects that is treated as a single unit for data manipulation and consistency management. An aggregate data model organises data as such units, so that each aggregate is stored, retrieved, updated and distributed as a whole (Sadalage and Fowler, NoSQL Distilled; the term comes from Domain-Driven Design).
  • Why aggregates: applications usually work with a whole order or a whole customer at once; relational models scatter it across many tables and rebuild it with joins.
  • Aggregate boundary is a design choice driven by how data is accessed: the same data could be one "customer with all orders" aggregate or separate customer and order aggregates. As the notes put it, aggregation is not a logical property of the data itself — it depends entirely on how an application intends to use that data.
  • Links between aggregates are stored as references by id (Order holds customerId), not nested copies.
  • Aggregate-oriented databases: key-value, document and column-family stores; they know (and are built around) the aggregate as their unit.
  • Aggregate-ignorant databases: relational and graph databases; they have no notion of aggregate boundaries, which is good when data is accessed in many different ways and relationships matter.

How each NoSQL type treats the aggregate

  • Key-value: aggregate is an opaque value under a key; the DB cannot see inside, so lookup is by key only. Aggregate can hold anything.
  • Document: aggregate is a visible document; DB understands its structure, so you can query fields and retrieve parts, but the document structure limits what can be stored.
  • Column-family: a two-level map: row key → column families → columns. The row is the aggregate; column families group parts of it that are read together (e.g. profile and orders of one customer).
  • Graph: breaks data into small nodes and edges and stresses relationships, so there is no aggregate unit (aggregate-ignorant); graph DBs typically support ACID transactions across many nodes.

Consequences of aggregate orientation

  • Atomicity per aggregate: updates to a single aggregate are atomic; transactions spanning several aggregates are generally not supported and must be handled by application code.
  • Natural unit of distribution: a whole aggregate is kept on one node (sharding) and copied as one unit (replication), so a read needs one node.
  • Fast for the designed access path (get order by id), but awkward for other paths (e.g. "total sales of a product across all orders" needs scanning many aggregates, often via MapReduce).
  • Denormalisation: some data is duplicated inside aggregates, so updates may need care. A concrete case of exactly this: the same customer address might be copied into the Customer aggregate's billing address, the Order aggregate's shipping address, and again inside the Order's payment details — one real-world value, copied into up to three places instead of referenced once.
Common mistake: some class notes state flatly that "NoSQL databases don't support ACID transactions". This is stated as an absolute and is too strong. Aggregate-oriented databases DO give ACID guarantees within one aggregate — the same notes say this a line later ("aggregate-oriented databases support the atomic manipulation of a single aggregate at a time") — and several modern products go further still: MongoDB has supported multi-document ACID transactions since version 4.0 (2018), and graph databases such as Neo4j are fully ACID compliant. The accurate, narrower claim is that NoSQL databases generally do not give cheap ACID transactions spanning many aggregates/rows at once (e.g. a banking-style transfer between two accounts) the way an RDBMS does.

1.16 Short answers (1 mark)

Q1. Define big data.
Data sets whose volume, velocity and variety exceed the capacity of traditional database systems to store, manage and analyse, requiring distributed and cost-effective processing to extract insight.
Q2. List the 5 Vs of big data.
Volume (amount), Velocity (speed of generation/processing), Variety (formats), Veracity (trustworthiness/quality), Value (usefulness). Some books add Variability and Visualization.
Q3. Differentiate structured and unstructured data.
Structured data has a fixed schema in rows and columns and is queried with SQL (e.g. bank table). Unstructured data has no predefined model and needs special analysis (e.g. images, videos, free text).
Q4. Name three sources of big data.
Human-generated (social media posts, emails), machine-generated (IoT sensors, web server logs, GPS) and organisational/transactional (sales, banking, ERP records).
Q5. Expand ETL and ELT.
ETL = Extract, Transform, Load (transform before loading, used with data warehouses). ELT = Extract, Load, Transform (load raw data first, transform inside the target, used with data lakes/big data).
Q6. Differentiate data lake and data warehouse.
A data warehouse stores processed, structured data with schema-on-write for reporting. A data lake stores raw data of all types with schema-on-read for exploration and data science.
Q7. What is Hadoop?
An open-source Apache framework for distributed storage (HDFS) and distributed processing (MapReduce, with YARN managing resources) of very large data sets on clusters of commodity hardware.
Q8. What is the role of the NameNode?
The master of HDFS; it stores metadata (namespace, file-to-block mapping, block locations, permissions) and manages DataNodes, but does not store actual data.
Q9. What is the default block size and replication factor in HDFS?
Block size is 128 MB in Hadoop 2.x/3.x (64 MB in Hadoop 1.x); default replication factor is 3. Both are configurable.
Q10. Expand YARN and name its components.
Yet Another Resource Negotiator. Components: ResourceManager (Scheduler + ApplicationsManager), NodeManager, ApplicationMaster and Container.
Q11. What happens in the shuffle and sort phase?
The framework transfers map outputs to reducers, sorts them by key and groups all values of the same key into a list, so each reducer receives (key, list of values).
Q12. Expand NoSQL and list its four types.
"Not only SQL". Types: key-value store (Redis), document store (MongoDB), column-family store (Cassandra, HBase), graph database (Neo4j).
Q13. State the CAP theorem.
A distributed data store cannot simultaneously guarantee Consistency, Availability and Partition tolerance; during a network partition it must give up either consistency or availability.
Q14. Expand BASE.
Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency: the relaxed consistency model used by many NoSQL systems, as opposed to ACID.
Q15. What is an aggregate in NoSQL?
A collection of related objects (e.g. an order with its items, shipping address and payment) treated as a single unit for storage, retrieval, updates and distribution.
Q16. Why is a graph database called aggregate-ignorant?
Because it splits data into many small nodes and edges focusing on relationships, with no notion of an aggregate boundary, like relational databases.
Q17. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of NoSQL.
Advantages: horizontal scaling on commodity servers; flexible schema for semi-structured data. Disadvantages: weaker (eventual) consistency; no standard query language and weak joins.
Q18. List the advantages of big data.
Better decision-making, reduced cost of business processes, fraud detection, increased productivity, improved customer service, and increased agility.
Q19. List the disadvantages/challenges of big data.
Lack of skilled talent, security risks from large pooled data sets, and compliance with data-protection/regulatory requirements.
Q20. Name four sectors that use big data applications.
Any four of: education, healthcare, government, media/entertainment, weather, transportation, banking, marketing, business insights, space.
Q21. Why does HDFS split a file into blocks?
Storing and seeking through one huge file on a single disk is slow; splitting it into fixed-size blocks (128 MB) lets many machines store and read/write parts in parallel, cutting seek time.
Q22. What are the fsimage and the edit log?
The fsimage is a snapshot of the NameNode's metadata at one point in time; the edit log records every change since. On restart the NameNode reloads the fsimage and replays the edit log to rebuild its current state.
Q23. List the four features of YARN.
Multi-tenancy (many engines share one cluster), Scalability, Cluster-utilisation (dynamic resource sharing), Compatibility (old MapReduce jobs still run).
Q24. What do RecordReader and OutputFormat do in MapReduce?
RecordReader converts an input split into (key, value) records for the Map function; OutputFormat (via a RecordWriter) writes the Reducer's final (key, value) pairs to HDFS, one record per line.
Q25. Is it correct to say NoSQL databases have no ACID support at all?
No, that is an oversimplification. Aggregate-oriented NoSQL databases are atomic within a single aggregate, and some products (e.g. MongoDB since v4.0) support multi-document ACID transactions. What NoSQL generally lacks is cheap ACID transactions spanning many aggregates/rows at once, unlike an RDBMS.

2.1 Data Science: Getting Value out of Big Data

Big data by itself is just a heap of bytes; value comes only when analysis gives an insight and someone acts on it. (data se insight, insight se action, action se value)

Definition: Data science is the process of using data (often big data from many sources) with statistics, computing and domain knowledge to produce insights that lead to informed actions, and therefore to business or scientific value.
  • Value is the goal: of the V's of big data, "value" is the reason we collect the other V's at all. (faayda nahi to data bekaar)
  • Integration creates value: combining diverse data (sales + weather + social media) reveals patterns no single source shows.
  • Insight = a new, useful understanding (e.g. "customers in rain order more hot food").
  • Action = a decision taken on the insight (e.g. push soup offers on rainy evenings).
  • Common value areas: personalised recommendations, sentiment analysis, fraud detection, predictive maintenance, better operations and planning.
  • Data science is a team activity: it needs domain experts, analysts and engineers together, guided by a clear question.

2.2 Building a Big Data Strategy

A strategy is the plan that says why the organisation uses big data, who does it and how it spreads through the company. (pehle goal, phir team, phir tools)

Definition: A big data strategy is an organisation's plan for using big data and analytics to meet clearly defined business objectives, covering the team, leadership buy-in, data sharing and policies, and an analytics-driven culture. The first step is always the business objective.
  • Business objectives: short-term and long-term goals, decided with all stakeholders and communicated to everyone. (kya paana hai, pehle likho)
  • Diverse team: domain experts + statisticians / ML people + data engineers + programmers; no single "unicorn" knows all.
  • Organisational buy-in: commitment and sponsorship from top leadership; without it projects die.
  • In-house expertise: constant training on new tools; an internal "idea lab" to try things.
  • Start small: pilot analytics on a small scale, prove value, then deploy widely.
  • Share data, remove silos: departments should not hide data from each other.
  • Policies: rules for privacy, data quality, security, ownership and legal regulation.
  • Culture: decisions based on data, not gut feeling; strategy is revised as business grows.
Example
Objective: an online grocery app wants to cut vegetable wastage.
Team: supply-chain expert + data analyst + data engineer; CEO sponsors it.
Pilot: demand forecasting in one city's warehouses first.
Share: sales, stock and weather data joined; policy: customer data anonymised.
If pilot reduces wastage, roll out to all cities and make forecasting part of daily planning.

Before building a strategy: four things to settle first

  • Clear objectives: a general understanding of what the project must achieve and which problem it solves.
  • Budget: a realistic estimate that also leaves room for contingencies.
  • Time frame: planned around resource availability and task dependencies; keep it flexible and avoid parallel changes.
  • Existing documentation: gathering what is already known about the current data and systems speeds up the start of the project.

Key components of a big data strategy (the document's own 5 parts — different from the 5 P's of data science in 2.4)

  • Business goals and objectives: specific, measurable targets (cut cost, raise satisfaction, find new revenue) that guide every later decision.
  • Prioritisation of use cases: a strategy can cover many use cases (predictive maintenance, sales forecasting, segmentation); tackle the ones with quick wins or the most pressing need first.
  • Data governance policy: who owns the data, what quality it must meet, and the rules for access and sharing — this is what stops data silos.
  • Data privacy and security needs: legal compliance (e.g. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, or GDPR/HIPAA abroad) is only the starting point; a full plan adds encryption, technical audits and an incident-response plan.
  • Data architecture: the choice between a data lake, a data warehouse or a hybrid, plus how data flows and scales.

Definition: The five P's of data science are Purpose, People, Process, Platforms and Programmability: the interdependent parts that together turn big data into a data product and value.

2.5 Steps in the Data Science Process

Every data science project follows five steps: Acquire, Prepare, Analyze, Report, Act, and then loops back with new questions. (lao, saaf karo, samjho, batao, karo)

Definition: The data science process is an iterative sequence of five steps: Acquire data, Prepare it (explore + pre-process), Analyze it (build and evaluate models), Report the insights, and Act on them.
  • 1. Acquire: identify suitable data sets and retrieve / query them. Four main source categories: traditional databases (SQL), text files (CSV, logs), remote data (web services / APIs), NoSQL storage. (data lao)
  • 2. Prepare has two parts:
    • Explore: understand the nature of data, preliminary analysis: summary statistics (mean, median, range), correlations, trends, outliers, plots.
    • Pre-process: clean (missing, invalid, duplicate values, outliers) and wrangle / munge (scaling, transformation, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, data manipulation such as merging).
  • 3. Analyze: select technique → build model → evaluate. Techniques: classification, regression, clustering, association analysis, graph analytics.
  • 4. Report: communicate results with visualisations; state main findings, their value and limits, to non-technical decision makers.
  • 5. Act: turn insight into action (deploy model, change business process), monitor results; new questions start the loop again.

2.6 Big Data Modeling and Management: Overview

Before analysis, big data must be brought in, stored, kept clean, operated on, scaled and protected. (data ko sambhalna hi management hai)

Definition: Big data management is the set of activities and systems for ingesting, storing, ensuring quality of, operating on, scaling and securing very large and varied data so it stays usable. A data model describes the data by three parts: structure, operations and constraints.
  • Data model: structure: how data is organised (table rows/columns, JSON document, graph nodes/edges).
  • Data model: operations: what you can do with it (select, join, aggregate).
  • Data model: constraints: rules data must obey (age ≥ 0, roll number unique).
  • Management questions: how does data get in (ingestion), where does it live (storage), is it correct (quality), how do we use it (operations), how does it grow (scalability), who may see it (security).

2.7 Data Ingestion

Ingestion is the front door: getting data from its sources into your data system, ideally automatically. (data ko system ke andar laana)

Definition: Data ingestion is the process of getting data from its sources into the data system being built or used (for storage and processing), either in batches or as a continuous stream, according to an ingestion policy.
  • Batch ingestion: data collected and loaded at intervals (e.g. nightly load of the day's sales). (ikattha karke ek saath)
  • Streaming (real-time) ingestion: each record flows in as it is produced (sensor readings, tweets, clicks). (lagatar behta hua)
  • Automation: at big data scale, ingestion must be automated, not manual uploads.
  • Infrastructure questions to ask before designing ingestion:
    • How many data sources are there, and will the number grow?
    • How large is each data item, and at what rate does data arrive?
    • What to do with bad data (reject, repair, or flag and keep)?
    • What to do when data arrives too fast / too much, or too little?
  • Ingestion policy / error policy: e.g. hospital medical records are never discarded; errors are flagged but data retained. For a high-volume social-media stream, bad records may simply be dropped.
  • Typical tools: Apache Kafka / Flume (streams, logs), Sqoop (relational DB to Hadoop).

Data Ingestion Techniques: ETL, ELT and CDC

  • Choice of technique depends on: the business requirement, the complexity of the data, and the infrastructure already in place.
  • ETL (Extract, Transform, Load): data is extracted, cleaned/transformed into a consistent format, then loaded into storage such as a data warehouse. Best when structured, ready-to-analyse data is the priority (reports, strict quality rules, relational databases).
  • ELT (Extract, Load, Transform): data is extracted and loaded into the target (a data lake / cloud platform) in raw form first, and transformed later, on demand, inside that system. Best for large volumes of unstructured / semi-structured data where transformation can wait.
  • CDC (Change Data Capture): detects and captures only the changes (insert / update / delete) made at a source and replicates just those changes to the target in real time, instead of reloading the whole dataset. Used for high-frequency trading, e-commerce, or keeping systems in sync with minimal lag.

Challenges in Data Ingestion

  • Handling large volumes: data keeps growing (especially real-time IoT / social-media streams), so the pipeline must scale continuously.

2.8 Data Storage

Storage decides where data lives: fast but small memory near the CPU, or slow but huge disks and archives. (tez aur chhota, ya dheema aur bada)

Definition: Data storage in big data management is the choice of devices and systems that hold data so that it can be retrieved efficiently, considering capacity, speed (memory hierarchy), cost and how storage scales.
  • Memory hierarchy: moving up = faster access, higher cost per GB, smaller size; moving down = the reverse.
  • Capacity: how much data now and how much after growth (estimate from ingestion rate, 2.7).
  • Volatile vs non-volatile: RAM loses data on power off; SSD, HDD, tape keep it.
  • Hot vs cold data: frequently used data on fast storage / in memory (in-memory systems like Spark cache); old data on cheap archive.
  • Scale up vs scale out: bigger storage in one machine vs more machines (distributed storage like HDFS). Detail in 2.11.
  • Storage choices: relational DB (structured), NoSQL (key-value, document, column, graph), distributed file system (HDFS), cloud object storage, data warehouse vs data lake.

Storage Models

  • File-based storage: HDFS is the backbone of Hadoop storage — large files are split into blocks spread across many nodes, replicated so no data is lost if a node fails.
  • NoSQL databases: built for scale and flexibility with semi-structured / unstructured data. Four types: key-value (Redis, DynamoDB), document (MongoDB), column-family (Cassandra, HBase) and graph (Neo4j) — for connected data such as social networks.
  • Cloud (object) storage: managed services that store petabytes with elasticity, paying only for what is used — e.g. AWS S3, Azure Data Lake, Google Cloud Storage.
  • Key features to check in any big data storage system: scalability (expands easily), fault tolerance (replication so failures don't lose data), high availability (data always reachable).

2.9 Data Quality

Garbage in, garbage out: a perfect model on dirty data still gives wrong answers. (ganda data, galat faisla)

Definition: Data quality is the degree to which data is accurate, complete, consistent, valid and timely for its intended use. Data cleaning is the process of detecting and fixing or removing dirty data (missing, invalid, duplicate, inconsistent values and outliers).
  • Why quality matters: better decisions and analytics, regulatory compliance (finance, health), avoids cost of wrong actions, builds trust in reports.
  • Big data makes it harder: many sources, fast arrival and variety mean more inconsistency (veracity problem).
  • Dirty data issues:
    • Missing values: empty field (no phone number).
    • Invalid values: impossible data (age = 250).
    • Duplicate records: same customer entered twice.
    • Inconsistent values: "Delhi", "New Delhi", "DEL"; different units (°C vs °F).
    • Outliers / noise: extreme values far from the rest.
  • Ways to address: remove records with missing values, generate best estimates (mean / median) for invalid or missing values, merge duplicate records, remove outliers, standardise formats and units.
  • Dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, validity, timeliness, uniqueness.
Example (body temperature readings, °C)
R1 = 36.6, R2 = 37.2, R3 = missing, R4 = 98.4, R5 = 36.9, R6 = duplicate of R2
R4 = 98.4 is invalid in °C (entered in °F) → remove (or convert); R6 → merge with R2
Valid values = 36.6, 37.2, 36.9 → mean = (36.6 + 37.2 + 36.9) / 3 = 110.7 / 3 = 36.9
Missing R3 ← estimate with mean 36.9 (1 decimal)
Clean set: R1 36.6, R2 37.2, R3 36.9 (estimated), R5 36.9

Data Quality Assessment Framework (DQAF)

  • DQAF (Data Quality Assessment Framework) rates a dataset on 6 dimensions, each typically scored 0-100: Completeness, Timeliness, Validity, Integrity, Uniqueness, Consistency.
  • Completeness: the percentage of the dataset that is missing.
  • Timeliness: whether the data is current enough for the decision (old customer data used today is a timeliness problem).
  • Validity: whether data follows the required policy / procedure / format (e.g. a birthday entered in the wrong format).
  • Integrity: whether the facts and figures are dependable and trustworthy.
  • Uniqueness: each real-world entity (e.g. a customer) is recorded only once.
  • Consistency: the same fact agrees wherever it is recorded, matching the collecting department's own goals.
Mnemonic DQAF = "C-T-V-I-U-C": Completeness, Timeliness, Validity, Integrity, Uniqueness, Consistency.
Common mistake: some data-quality frameworks name the sixth dimension "Accuracy" instead of "Integrity" — both mean the same thing (the facts are correct/dependable). Use the name your own notes give (Integrity, here) but recognise "Accuracy" as the same idea if a question uses that word instead.

Good Data Quality, Its Benefits, and Its Challenges

  • Good data quality has 4 aspects:
    • Accuracy: error-free, so it can be relied on for decisions.
    • Completeness: has everything needed for the task at hand.
    • Relevance: fits the current goal — outdated or irrelevant data misleads.
    • Consolidation: free of duplicates — each fact stored only once.
  • Benefits of high quality: lets a business build reliable KPIs and out-perform rivals, and lets teams pinpoint exactly where an operational workflow is failing.
  • Challenges in data quality:
    • Incomplete data: hard to collect fully accurate, gap-free data.
    • Data accuracy issues: inconsistent sources, input mistakes, system faults.
    • Data integration complexity: merging many sources cleanly is hard.
    • Data governance: needs clear ownership, policies and stewardship.
  • How to improve quality (6 steps): define standards → data profiling (study structure/patterns/anomalies) → validation rules at entry → data governance (policies, steward roles) → regular audits → automated monitoring (continuous tracking with alerts).
Mnemonic Good quality = "ACRC": Accuracy, Completeness, Relevance, Consolidation. Improve it = "D-P-V-G-R-A": Define standards, Profile, Validate at entry, Govern, Regular audits, Automate monitoring.
Mnemonic Dirty data "MIDIO" = Missing, Invalid, Duplicate, Inconsistent, Outliers.
Common mistake: always deleting outliers. Some outliers are real events (a genuine fraud); check before removing.
Quick recall Quality = accurate, complete, consistent, valid, timely; clean MIDIO by remove / estimate / merge / standardise. DQAF scores 6 dimensions (Completeness, Timeliness, Validity, Integrity, Uniqueness, Consistency); good quality = ACRC; improve with define → profile → validate → govern → audit → automate.

2.10 Data Operations

Operations are the actions you perform on data: on one item (crop one photo) or on a whole collection (filter, join, count). (data par kaam: ek par ya poore samooh par)

Definition: Data operations are the procedures a data model allows for retrieving and manipulating data. They act either on a single data item or on a collection of items (selection/filtering, projection, join, union, aggregation, sorting), and at big data scale they are run in parallel.
  • Single-item operations: work on one record / object: look up a record, update a field, resize or crop one image.
  • Selection (filtering / subsetting): keep only items that satisfy a condition (orders with amount ≥ 200). (shart par chhaanto)
  • Projection (substructure extraction): keep only some attributes (just name and city columns).
  • Join: combine two collections on a common key (orders + customers on customer_id).
  • Union: merge two collections of the same structure (Jan sales ∪ Feb sales).
  • Aggregation: compute summary values over groups: count, sum, average, min, max.
  • Sorting / ranking: order results (top 10 products).
  • Big data aspect: collection operations are split over many machines (e.g. MapReduce: map filters/transforms, reduce aggregates).
Example (orders collection: city, amount in Rs)
Data: (Delhi,250), (Pune,400), (Delhi,150), (Pune,100), (Delhi,300)
Selection amount ≥ 200 → (Delhi,250), (Pune,400), (Delhi,300)
Aggregation SUM by city on all orders → Delhi = 250+150+300 = 700; Pune = 400+100 = 500
COUNT by city → Delhi 3, Pune 2; AVG → Delhi 700/3 = 233.33, Pune 500/2 = 250.00 (2 decimals)
Selection gives 3 orders; totals Delhi 700, Pune 500; averages 233.33 and 250.00.

DataOps: Operating the Data Pipeline Itself

The class notes also use "data operations" for a second, different idea: DataOps, a DevOps-style way of running the whole data pipeline so it stays fast, tested and trustworthy — not the select/join/aggregate operations above. (pipeline ko bhi DevOps jaisa chalao)

Definition: DataOps is an Agile approach, borrowed from DevOps, for building and running the end-to-end data pipeline so that big data reaches business decisions faster, with better quality and predictability.
  • Why DataOps matters: rapid experimentation, collaboration across the whole data lifecycle, high quality with low error rates, data transparency, and simpler, continuous delivery of insight.
  • Automated testing: automated tests validate the data entering the system, and the inputs, outputs and business logic at every transformation step — a bad record is caught right where it is introduced, not after the report is built.
  • Monitoring: Statistical Process Control (SPC) continuously watches the pipeline's data flow and flags it the moment something drifts from normal, so the system is always known to be working as intended.
  • Technology-agnostic: DataOps is a way of working, not one tool or framework — it can be built with whatever pipeline tools an organisation already has.
  • Benefits: better cooperation between development, operations and data teams; higher efficiency through automation; better-quality data (errors caught early); better use of resources.
  • Costs: integrating many different tools can be hard, more automation needs more security care, the change is complex to roll out, and it needs upfront investment in tooling and training.
Mnemonic Collection operations "SPJUA": "Sab Pizza Jaldi Uthao Abhi" = Selection, Projection, Join, Union, Aggregation.
Mnemonic DataOps = Agile + DevOps for data: automated test → deploy → monitor, on repeat.
Common mistake: mixing selection with projection: selection picks rows, projection picks columns.
Quick recall Single item vs collection; collection ops = select, project, join, union, aggregate (run in parallel for big data). DataOps = Agile/DevOps-style pipeline operation: automated testing + SPC monitoring keep the pipeline fast and trustworthy.

2.11 Data Scalability and Security

Scalability means the system keeps working as data grows; security means only the right people see and change it. (badhe to sambhale, aur chori na ho)

Definition: Data scalability is the ability of a data system to handle growing data volume and workload by adding resources, either by scaling up (a more powerful machine) or scaling out (more machines). Data security is protecting data from unauthorised access, change or loss through access control, encryption, secure transmission and privacy measures.
  • Scale up (vertical): add CPU / RAM / disk to one server. Simple, no code change, but costly and has a hardware ceiling and a single point of failure.
  • Scale out (horizontal): add more ordinary machines to a cluster (Hadoop, NoSQL). Near-unlimited growth, cheaper hardware, but needs distributed software. (ek bada nahi, kai chhote)
  • Partitioning (sharding): split data into parts stored on different nodes so work runs in parallel (HDFS blocks; shards by user_id).
  • Replication: keep copies of each part on several nodes for fault tolerance and faster reads (HDFS default replication factor = 3).
  • Elasticity: in the cloud, add or remove nodes as load changes.

  • Access control: authentication (prove identity: password, OTP) + authorisation (roles/permissions: analyst can read, not delete).
  • Encryption: convert data to ciphertext so a stolen disk or file is useless without the key; costs extra CPU time for encrypt/decrypt, which matters at big data scale.
  • Secure transmission: data moving between nodes or to users must travel encrypted (TLS), since big data systems send data across networks constantly.
  • Privacy: personal data (Aadhaar, health records) must be anonymised or masked and used only with consent / as law allows.
  • More nodes, more risk: scale out spreads data over many machines, so every node and link must be secured.

Scalability: Key Aspects and Enabling Technologies

  • Key aspects: handling volume (ever-growing datasets), performance (stay fast and reliable as load rises), flexibility (adapt to new data types and demands over time).
  • Enabling technologies: cloud computing (e.g. AWS S3 for elastic storage), distributed computing (Hadoop, Spark processing data in parallel across servers), flexible storage (mixing relational + NoSQL + object storage for different needs), and virtualisation / containerisation (using physical hardware resources more effectively).

Security: the CIA Triad and Data Loss Prevention

  • CIA triad: every security control ultimately protects Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability together — the standard three goals of data security.
  • Data Loss Prevention (DLP): systems and policies that stop sensitive data from leaving the organisation's control (e.g. blocking an unauthorised copy or upload of a customer file) — a control alongside access control, encryption and auditing.
  • Scalability vs security trade-off: encryption and access checks add processing overhead, so both must be designed in from the start, tracked with continuous monitoring, and built with adaptable, modular architectures rather than bolted on afterwards.
Mnemonic Scalability "UP-OUT-PR": scale UP, scale OUT, Partition, Replicate. Security "AESPA": Access control, Encryption, Secure transmission, Privacy, Auditing.
Mnemonic Security goals = "CIA": Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — DLP is an extra control on top of AESPA that stops sensitive data leaving the organisation.
Common mistake: thinking replication is only for backup. It also allows reads from several nodes, improving speed and availability.
Quick recall Scale up = bigger box, scale out = more boxes (+ partition, replicate); key aspects = volume, performance, flexibility. Security = CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity, availability) delivered by access control, encryption (rest + transit), privacy, auditing and DLP.

2.15 Short answers (1 mark)

Q1. Define data science.
Using data with statistics, computing and domain knowledge to produce insights that lead to actions and value. Chain: data → analysis → insight → action → value.
Q2. How is value obtained from big data?
By integrating diverse data, analysing it to get insight, and acting on that insight (e.g. fraud detection, recommendations). Size alone gives no value.
Q3. What is the first step of a big data strategy?
Defining business objectives (short and long term) with stakeholders; then team, buy-in, expertise, pilot, data sharing with policies, and analytics culture.
Q4. List the five P's of data science.
Purpose, People, Process, Platforms, Programmability. They are also called the five components of data science.
Q5. What is "Programmability" in the 5 P's?
The ability to program the analysis with languages and tools (Python, R, SQL, MapReduce, workflows) so steps are repeatable and scalable.
Q6. List the steps of the data science process.
Acquire, Prepare (explore + pre-process), Analyze, Report, Act. The process is iterative.
Q7. What happens in the Prepare step?
Explore: understand the nature of data by preliminary analysis (statistics, plots). Pre-process: clean dirty data and wrangle it (scaling, transformation, feature selection).
Q8. What are the steps in Analyze?
Select technique (classification, regression, clustering etc.), build model, evaluate model.
Q9. Define data ingestion.
Getting data from its sources into the data system for storage and processing, in batches or as a stream, under an ingestion policy.
Q10. Differentiate batch and streaming ingestion.
Batch loads large chunks periodically with high latency (nightly sales). Streaming loads records continuously with low latency (sensor data, fraud alerts).
Q11. What is the memory hierarchy?
Ordering of storage by speed and cost: cache → RAM → SSD → HDD → tape. Going down: slower, larger, cheaper per GB.
Q12. Name four dirty-data issues.
Missing values, invalid values, duplicate records, inconsistent values (also outliers).
Q13. Differentiate selection and projection.
Selection picks rows that satisfy a condition; projection picks chosen columns (attributes) of each row.
Q14. What is aggregation?
A collection operation computing summary values (sum, count, average, min, max) over groups, e.g. total sales per city.
Q15. Differentiate scale up and scale out.
Scale up: a more powerful single machine (hardware ceiling). Scale out: add more commodity machines to a cluster (partitioning + replication).
Q16. List data security measures.
Access control (authentication, authorisation), encryption at rest, secure transmission (TLS), privacy (anonymisation, masking), auditing.
Q17. What are the three components of a data model?
Structure (organisation of data), operations (what can be done), constraints (rules data must obey).
Q18. What is a big data management plan?
A design stating how data is ingested, stored, kept at good quality, processed (operations), scaled and secured for an organisation's data.
Q19. Why is a data lake used for customer data from web, app and social media?
It stores raw data of every type (structured, JSON, text, images) cheaply at scale, with schema applied only when data is read.
Q20. What is collaborative filtering?
A recommendation technique that suggests items liked by users with similar behaviour, measured with a similarity such as cosine similarity.
Q21. What is A/B testing?
Showing two versions to two random groups of users and keeping the version that performs better on a chosen metric.
Q22. What should be settled before building a big data strategy?
Clear objectives, an approximate budget (with room for contingencies), a realistic time frame, and documentation of the existing data/systems.
Q23. List the five components of a big data strategy document.
Business goals & objectives, prioritisation of use cases, data governance policy, data privacy & security needs, data architecture. (Different from the 5 P's of data science.)
Q24. Name any three factors that affect how long a big data strategy takes.
Team size, volume/complexity of data, and regulatory/compliance requirements (also: technical debt, infrastructure specifics).
Q25. Give an alternate "five components of data science" answer built from the process.
Data collection, data cleaning, EDA & visualisation, data modeling, model evaluation & deployment — the data science process (2.5) relabelled as components.
Q26. What is CRISP-DM?
A widely used 6-stage cyclic data-mining process: business understanding, data understanding, data preparation, modeling, evaluation, deployment — the same idea as the 5-step Acquire-Prepare-Analyze-Report-Act process at finer granularity.
Q27. What did the real-estate case study predict, and with which tools?
Which homeowners were about to sell their house, using Amazon S3 for storage and AWS Glue for data processing; the case study reports about 70% prediction accuracy.
Q28. What is a DQAF?
Data Quality Assessment Framework: scores a dataset on 6 dimensions — completeness, timeliness, validity, integrity, uniqueness, consistency — each typically rated 0-100.
Q29. What are the 4 aspects of good data quality?
Accuracy, Completeness, Relevance, Consolidation (ACRC) — error-free, nothing needed missing, fits the task, no duplicates.
Q30. Differentiate ETL and ELT.
ETL transforms data before loading it into a warehouse (structured, strict schema). ELT loads raw data first into a lake / cloud platform and transforms it later, on demand.
Q31. What is Change Data Capture (CDC)?
A technique that captures only the inserts/updates/deletes made at a source and replicates just those changes to the target in real time, instead of reloading the whole dataset.
Q32. What is DataOps?
An Agile, DevOps-inspired approach to building and running the data pipeline (automated testing, monitoring, fast delivery) so big data reaches decisions faster and more reliably.
Q33. What is Data Loss Prevention (DLP)?
Systems and policies that stop sensitive data from leaving an organisation's control, e.g. blocking an unauthorised export of customer files.
Q34. What is the CIA triad in data security?
Confidentiality (only authorised access), Integrity (no unauthorised change), Availability (accessible when needed) — the three goals of data security.
Q35. Name the four NoSQL storage types.
Key-value (Redis, DynamoDB), document (MongoDB), column-family (Cassandra, HBase), graph (Neo4j).
Q36. Give two examples of cloud object storage.
AWS S3 and Azure Data Lake / Google Cloud Storage — managed, elastic services billed by usage.

Q8. A company collects customer information from websites, applications and social media. Design a Big Data Management Plan.

  1. State assumptions: sources, data types, about 5 million events/day at 2 KB (10 GB/day, about 11 TB/year with replication 3).
  2. Draw Fig 2.26 (sources → ingestion → storage → quality → operations → reports, wrapped by scalability and security).
  3. Ingestion: streaming for clicks and app events, batch for orders and social exports, buffer with a message queue.
  4. Storage: data lake for raw data, NoSQL for profiles and events, warehouse for reports.
  5. Data quality: dedupe across channels, validate, standardise, missing values, outliers, entity resolution.
  6. Operations: filter, join, aggregate, top-N, sentiment analysis, 360° customer view.
  7. Scalability: scale out, partitioning, replication, auto-scaling on sale days.
  8. Security: encryption, Kerberos + role-based access, masking, audit, consent and retention (DPDP Act, 2023). Conclude with the business outcome.
