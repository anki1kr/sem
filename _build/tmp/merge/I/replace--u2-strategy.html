2.2 Building a Big Data Strategy

A strategy is the plan that says why the organisation uses big data, who does it and how it spreads through the company. (pehle goal, phir team, phir tools)

Definition: A big data strategy is an organisation's plan for using big data and analytics to meet clearly defined business objectives, covering the team, leadership buy-in, data sharing and policies, and an analytics-driven culture. The first step is always the business objective.
  • Business objectives: short-term and long-term goals, decided with all stakeholders and communicated to everyone. (kya paana hai, pehle likho)
  • Diverse team: domain experts + statisticians / ML people + data engineers + programmers; no single "unicorn" knows all.
  • Organisational buy-in: commitment and sponsorship from top leadership; without it projects die.
  • In-house expertise: constant training on new tools; an internal "idea lab" to try things.
  • Start small: pilot analytics on a small scale, prove value, then deploy widely.
  • Share data, remove silos: departments should not hide data from each other.
  • Policies: rules for privacy, data quality, security, ownership and legal regulation.
  • Culture: decisions based on data, not gut feeling; strategy is revised as business grows.
Example
Objective: an online grocery app wants to cut vegetable wastage.
Team: supply-chain expert + data analyst + data engineer; CEO sponsors it.
Pilot: demand forecasting in one city's warehouses first.
Share: sales, stock and weather data joined; policy: customer data anonymised.
If pilot reduces wastage, roll out to all cities and make forecasting part of daily planning.

Before building a strategy: four things to settle first

  • Clear objectives: a general understanding of what the project must achieve and which problem it solves.
  • Budget: a realistic estimate that also leaves room for contingencies.
  • Time frame: planned around resource availability and task dependencies; keep it flexible and avoid parallel changes.
  • Existing documentation: gathering what is already known about the current data and systems speeds up the start of the project.

Key components of a big data strategy (the document's own 5 parts — different from the 5 P's of data science in 2.4)

  • Business goals and objectives: specific, measurable targets (cut cost, raise satisfaction, find new revenue) that guide every later decision.
  • Prioritisation of use cases: a strategy can cover many use cases (predictive maintenance, sales forecasting, segmentation); tackle the ones with quick wins or the most pressing need first.
  • Data governance policy: who owns the data, what quality it must meet, and the rules for access and sharing — this is what stops data silos.
  • Data privacy and security needs: legal compliance (e.g. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, or GDPR/HIPAA abroad) is only the starting point; a full plan adds encryption, technical audits and an incident-response plan.
  • Data architecture: the choice between a data lake, a data warehouse or a hybrid, plus how data flows and scales.
Mnemonic "Big Plates Get Pretty Aloo" = Business goals, Prioritisation, Governance, Privacy & security, Architecture.

A detailed 7-step roadmap (as named in the class notes)

Fig 2.2 above gives the general idea; the notes also name a more detailed, numbered 7-step roadmap for building the strategy document itself.

  • 1. Understand business objectives: SWOT analysis, market needs, KPIs, short/long-term goals, stakeholder alignment, risk assessment.
  • 2. Initial assessment and scoping: study the current data landscape and tools, fix the project's scope, review compliance needs, set milestones.
  • 3. Connect the data strategy with the business strategy: align data metrics with business KPIs so every department stays in agreement.
  • 4. Identify big data sources: list internal and external sources and web/app analytics, and decide real-time vs batch.
  • 5. Create a data strategy roadmap: a guiding document (executive summary, situational analysis, strategic objectives, tactical plans, implementation timeline, monitoring/evaluation, next steps) usually spanning 1-5 years.
  • 6. Set up monitoring and iteration: define analytical objectives, build analytical pipelines, add quality checks, plan regular review.
  • 7. Plan intelligent, data-driven decisions: define a decision framework, use predictive analytics, choose visualisation tools, automate decisions.
Mnemonic "Odd Aunts Cook Idli, Relish Mango Dosa" = Objectives, Assessment, Connect, Identify (sources), Roadmap, Monitor, Decide.

Factors affecting how long it takes

  • Team size
  • Volume and complexity of data
  • Potential data issues and technical debt
  • Infrastructure specificities
  • Regulatory / compliance requirements
  • Typical duration: the source notes report that such a project "may take 3-6 months or more" when run by specialised scientists and engineers — this is the case study's own estimate, not a universal rule.

Real-world proof: a real-estate case study

Case study (as reported in the source notes)
Challenge: predict which homeowners are about to sell their house, before the property is even listed.
Data: the company tracked over 1,400 property and demographic attributes across a dataset of more than 60 million records.
Solution: raw data re-uploaded to Amazon S3 storage, then transformed and processed with AWS Glue.
Reported result: the model could predict at least about 70% of the homeowners who were going to sell — the case study describes this as well above the industry average.
Mnemonic "Only Tall Boys Enjoy Tasty Samosa Cutlets" = Objectives, Team, Buy-in, Expertise, Try small, Share + policies, Culture.
Common mistake: starting with "buy Hadoop" (technology first). Strategy is business-first; tools come after the objective.
Quick recall Objectives → Team → Buy-in → Expertise → Try small → Share/Policies → Culture, and keep revising. Strategy's 5 components: goals, prioritisation, governance, privacy/security, architecture.
