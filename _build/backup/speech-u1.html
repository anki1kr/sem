1.1 Speech Production Mechanism & Source-Filter Model

Human speech is produced when air pushed from the lungs is shaped by the vibrating vocal folds and acoustic resonance of the vocal tract. (fephdo ki hawa vocal tract se hokar aawaz banti hai)

Definition: Speech production is the physiological process where airflow generated by the lungs passes through the vocal cords (larynx) to create an acoustic excitation source, which is then filtered by the vocal tract cavity (pharynx, oral, and nasal cavities) to produce spoken sounds.
  • Voiced Sounds: Produced when vocal cords vibrate quasi-periodically (e.g., vowels /a/, /e/, /i/). Has a distinct fundamental frequency / pitch period $T_0$.
  • Unvoiced Sounds: Produced when vocal cords remain open and air turbulence creates noise at constriction points (e.g., fricatives /s/, /f/, /sh/). No vocal cord vibration.
  • Plosive Sounds: Produced by complete closure followed by sudden air release (e.g., /p/, /t/, /k/).
Sound TypeVocal Cord StateExcitation NatureSpectrum CharacteristicsTypical Examples
VoicedVibrating (oscillating)Periodic impulse trainHarmonic peaks (formants)Vowels (/a/, /o/), Nasals (/m/)
UnvoicedOpen (relaxed)Random white noiseFlat noise-like spectrumFricatives (/s/, /f/, /sh/)

Source-Filter Model of Speech Production:

Definition: The Source-Filter Model represents speech $s[n]$ as the output of a linear time-varying vocal tract filter $H(z)$ excited by either a periodic impulse train (for voiced speech) or white noise (for unvoiced speech).
Quick recall Speech = Source (glottal excitation) × Filter (vocal tract $H(z)$). Voiced = periodic impulses, Unvoiced = random noise.

1.2 Human Auditory System & Perception

The human ear converts mechanical acoustic waves into frequency-analyzed neural impulses via the cochlea. (kaam kahan se chalu hota hai: ear drum se nerve tak)

Definition: The Human Auditory System processes acoustic sound pressure waves through three main anatomical regions: the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear (cochlea), mapping frequencies along the basilar membrane for neural perception.
  • Human Hearing Frequency Range: Standard range is $20 \text{ Hz}$ to $20,000 \text{ Hz}$ ($20 \text{ kHz}$). Most sensitive between $1 \text{ kHz}$ and $4 \text{ kHz}$.
  • Telephone Bandwidth: Standard speech band for telephony is $300 \text{ Hz}$ to $3400 \text{ Hz}$ (sampled at $8 \text{ kHz}$ in narrowband PCM).
  • Cochlea & Basilar Membrane: The cochlea acts as a biological frequency spectrum analyzer. High frequencies resonate near the stiff base; low frequencies resonate near the flexible apex.
  • Auditory Masking: Phenomenon where a louder sound renders a softer adjacent frequency sound inaudible (frequential/simultaneous masking or temporal masking). Exploited in perceptual codecs.
Quick recall Hearing range = 20 Hz to 20 kHz; Telephone speech band = 300-3400 Hz (8 kHz sampling). Cochlea performs frequency decomposition along the basilar membrane.

1.3 General Structure of Speech Coders

A speech coder compresses digital speech into a compact bitstream for bandwidth-efficient transmission over channels. (speech ko kam bits me convert karna)

Definition: A speech coder consists of an encoder that analyzes and quantizes input speech parameters into a digital bitstream, and a decoder that synthesizes reconstructed speech from the received bitstream.
  • Speech Analysis: Extracts key features such as pitch period, linear prediction coefficients (LPC), frame energy, and spectral parameters.
  • Quantization & Encoding: Converts continuous parameter values into discrete binary index representations.
  • Speech Synthesis: Uses decoded parameters to reconstruct speech approximations via source-filter synthesis models.
Quick recall Speech Coder = Encoder (Analysis + Quantization) + Channel + Decoder (Synthesis).

Speech codecs balance bit-rate efficiency against synthetic speech naturalness across three distinct architectural families. (waveform, parametric aur hybrid me farak)

Definition: Speech coding techniques are classified based on operational mechanisms into: (1) Waveform Coders, (2) Parametric Coders (Vocoders), and (3) Hybrid Coders.

1.5 Requirements of Speech Codecs

Designing an optimal speech codec involves balancing four competing parameters: bit rate, speech quality, delay, and computational complexity. (chaar cheezon ke beech balance)

Definition: The performance of a speech codec is governed by four core requirements: low bit rate, high perceptual speech quality, minimal processing delay, and high noise/channel error robustness.

Mean Opinion Score (MOS) Scale for Speech Quality:

MOS ScoreQuality RatingUser PerceptionCodec Benchmark Example
5ExcellentImperceptible distortion, crystal clearUncompressed Audio
4Good (Toll Quality)Just perceptible distortion, acceptable for phone callsPCM G.711 (64 kbps), CELP G.729 (8 kbps)
3FairPerceptible distortion, requires slight effortADPCM G.726 @ lower bitrates
2PoorAnnoying distortion, considerable effort requiredLPC-10 (2.4 kbps)
1UnacceptableExtremely degraded, unintelligibleSevere packet loss / corrupted bitstream
  • Coding Delays:
    • Algorithmic / Frame Delay: Time required to buffer a speech frame (typically 10 ms – 30 ms) plus look-ahead.
    • Processing Delay: Computation time taken by DSP/CPU to run encoder algorithms.
    • Transmission Delay: Time spent transmitting encoded bits over network channels.
    • Why low delay matters: High delay (> 150 ms) creates annoying echo and disrupts natural two-way conversational turn-taking.
  • Robustness: Ability to maintain intelligibility despite channel bit errors, dropped packets (IP networks), and background noise (car/street noise).
Quick recall Key codec metrics: Bit rate (kbps), MOS quality scale (1-5, Toll quality = MOS 4.0), Delay (< 50 ms ideal for conversation), and Robustness.

1.6 Pitch-Period Estimation

Pitch period represents the fundamental repetition time of quasi-periodic vocal fold vibration during voiced speech. (pitched sound ki repetition cycle count karna)

Definition: Pitch-period estimation (pitch tracking) is the process of determining the fundamental period $T_0$ (or fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$) of a voiced speech segment.
  • Typical Fundamental Frequency ($F_0$) Ranges:
    • Adult Males: $80 \text{ Hz}$ to $160 \text{ Hz}$ ($T_0 \approx 6.25 \text{ ms}$ to $12.5 \text{ ms}$)
    • Adult Females: $150 \text{ Hz}$ to $300 \text{ Hz}$ ($T_0 \approx 3.33 \text{ ms}$ to $6.67 \text{ ms}$)
    • Children: $200 \text{ Hz}$ to $500 \text{ Hz}$ ($T_0 \approx 2.0 \text{ ms}$ to $5.0 \text{ ms}$)
  • Autocorrelation Method: Computes self-similarity of speech signal $x[n]$ across time lag $k$: \[ R[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] x[n+k] \] For voiced speech, $R[k]$ exhibits a prominent secondary peak at lag $k = P_0$, where pitch period $T_0 = P_0 / f_s$.
  • AMDF (Average Magnitude Difference Function): Alternative low-complexity pitch detector that searches for dips/minima: \[ D[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} |x[n] - x[n+k]| \]
Worked Example: Pitch Period Autocorrelation Estimation
Given synthetic periodic signal sequence ($N=12$): x = [2, 4, 1, -1, 2, 4, 1, -1, 2, 4, 1, -1]
Compute autocorrelation values $R[k] = \sum x[n] x[n+k]$ for $k = 0 \dots 5$:
$R[0] = 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 = 66.0$
$R[1] = 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) = 29.0$
$R[2] = 2(1) + 4(-1) + 1(2) + (-1)(4) + 2(1) + 4(-1) + 1(2) + (-1)(4) + 2(1) + 4(-1) = -10.0$
$R[3] = 2(-1) + 4(2) + 1(4) + (-1)(1) + 2(-1) + 4(2) + 1(4) + (-1)(1) + 2(-1) = 16.0$
$R[4] = 2(2) + 4(4) + 1(1) + (-1)(-1) + 2(2) + 4(4) + 1(1) + (-1)(-1) = 44.0$
$R[5] = 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) = 20.0$
Peak search for $k > 0$: Maximum peak occurs at lag $k = 4$ with $R[4] = 44.0$.
Detected Pitch Period $P_0 = 4$ samples ($T_0 = 4 / f_s$)
Quick recall Voiced speech is quasi-periodic; pitch lag $P_0$ corresponds to the major non-zero peak of the autocorrelation function $R[k]$.

1.7 All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) Linear Filters

Vocal tract resonances (formants) are modeled as all-pole IIR filters, while anti-resonances (nasals) use all-zero FIR components. (pole = resonance, zero = anti-resonance)

Definition: An all-pole filter (IIR) models feedback resonances with transfer function $H(z) = \frac{1}{A(z)}$, whereas an all-zero filter (FIR) models feedforward zeroes with $H(z) = B(z)$.
  • All-Zero Filter (FIR / Moving Average): \[ y[n] = b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] + \dots + b_m x[n-m] \] Transfer function: $H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} b_k z^{-k}$. Always stable.
  • All-Pole Filter (IIR / Autoregressive): \[ y[n] = x[n] - \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i y[n-i] \] Transfer function: $H(z) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i z^{-i}}$. Represents vocal tract resonances (formants).
Worked Example: All-Pole vs All-Zero Filter Impulse Responses
1. All-Zero FIR Filter: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.5 x[n-1] - 0.2 x[n-2]$
Impulse input $x[n] = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]$:
$y[0] = 1.0$, $y[1] = 0.5$, $y[2] = -0.2$, $y[3] = 0.0$, $y[4] = 0.0$.
2. All-Pole IIR Filter: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.6 y[n-1] - 0.15 y[n-2]$
Impulse input $x[n] = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]$:
$y[0] = 1.0$
$y[1] = 0.6(1.0) - 0.15(0) = 0.6$
$y[2] = 0.6(0.6) - 0.15(1.0) = 0.36 - 0.15 = 0.21$
$y[3] = 0.6(0.21) - 0.15(0.6) = 0.126 - 0.09 = 0.036$
$y[4] = 0.6(0.036) - 0.15(0.21) = 0.0216 - 0.0315 = -0.0099$
FIR output finite = [1.0, 0.5, -0.2, 0, 0]; IIR output infinite decaying = [1.0, 0.6, 0.21, 0.036, -0.0099]
Quick recall All-pole filters model vocal tract formants ($H(z) = 1/A(z)$); all-zero filters model zeroes ($H(z) = B(z)$).

1.8 Discrete-Time Convolution

Convolution combines an input signal with a filter's impulse response to compute the filtered output sound. (flip, shift, multiply aur add karo)

Definition: Discrete-time convolution of an input sequence $x[n]$ with an impulse response $h[n]$ produces an output sequence $y[n]$ defined by: \[ y[n] = (x * h)[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] h[n-k] \]
Worked Example: Computation of Discrete Convolution
Given sequences: $x[n] = [1, 2, 3]$ (length $L=3$), $h[n] = [2, 1]$ (length $M=2$).
Output length $N = L + M - 1 = 3 + 2 - 1 = 4$ samples ($n = 0 \dots 3$).
$y[0] = x[0]h[0] = 1 \times 2 = 2$
$y[1] = x[0]h[1] + x[1]h[0] = 1(1) + 2(2) = 1 + 4 = 5$
$y[2] = x[1]h[1] + x[2]h[0] = 2(1) + 3(2) = 2 + 6 = 8$
$y[3] = x[2]h[1] = 3 \times 1 = 3$
Convolved Output y[n] = [2, 5, 8, 3]
Quick recall Convolution formula $y[n] = \sum x[k] h[n-k]$; output length is $L + M - 1$.

1.9 Power Spectral Density, Periodogram, Autoregressive Model & Autocorrelation

Spectral analysis transforms time-domain speech samples into frequency domain energy distributions. (frequency domain me energy distribution dekho)

Definition: Power Spectral Density (PSD) represents how signal power is distributed across frequency components, defined via the Wiener-Khinchin theorem as the Fourier Transform of the autocorrelation sequence $R[k]$.
  • Periodogram: Direct nonparametric PSD estimator given by: \[ P(f) = \frac{1}{N} \left| X(f) \right|^2 = \frac{1}{N} \left| \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-j 2 \pi f n} \right|^2 \] Drawbacks: High variance and spectral leakage due to finite rectangular windowing. Solved using Hamming windowing.
  • Autoregressive (AR) Model: Parametric model where current sample $x[n]$ is expressed as a linear combination of past samples plus an error excitation $e[n]$: \[ x[n] = -\sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i x[n-i] + e[n] \] Directly forms the mathematical foundation of All-Pole filter modeling and Linear Predictive Coding (LPC).
  • Autocorrelation Estimation Formulas:
    • Biased Estimator: $R_{biased}[k] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] x[n+k]$ (Guarantees positive semi-definite spectrum).
    • Unbiased Estimator: $R_{unbiased}[k] = \frac{1}{N-k} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] x[n+k]$.
Worked Example: Biased vs Unbiased Autocorrelation Estimation
Given short sequence ($N=5$): x = [3, 1, -2, 4, 2]
Compute $R[0], R[1], R[2]$:
Sum for $k=0$: $3^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2 + 4^2 + 2^2 = 9 + 1 + 4 + 16 + 4 = 34$
Sum for $k=1$: $3(1) + 1(-2) + (-2)(4) + 4(2) = 3 - 2 - 8 + 8 = 1$
Sum for $k=2$: $3(-2) + 1(4) + (-2)(2) = -6 + 4 - 4 = -6$
Biased ($/N=5$): $R_{biased}[0] = 34/5 = 6.8$, $R_{biased}[1] = 1/5 = 0.2$, $R_{biased}[2] = -6/5 = -1.2$
Unbiased ($/(N-k)$): $R_{unbiased}[0] = 34/5 = 6.8$, $R_{unbiased}[1] = 1/4 = 0.25$, $R_{unbiased}[2] = -6/3 = -2.0$
Biased = [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]; Unbiased = [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]
Quick recall Wiener-Khinchin: PSD is Fourier Transform of autocorrelation. AR model $x[n] = -\sum a_i x[n-i] + e[n]$ provides smooth spectral envelope.

1.10 Short answers (1.5 marks)

1. Differentiate voiced and unvoiced speech sounds.
Voiced sounds are produced by vibrating vocal cords giving periodic impulse excitation (vowels); unvoiced sounds use open vocal cords producing random noise excitation (fricatives /s/).
2. What is the Source-Filter Model of speech production?
Model representing speech as excitation source (periodic impulses or noise) filtered by a linear system $H(z)$ representing vocal tract acoustic resonance.
3. What is the fundamental role of the cochlea in the auditory system?
The cochlea acts as a biological frequency spectrum analyzer, vibrating along the basilar membrane according to sound frequency.
4. State the standard human hearing and telephone speech frequency bands.
Human hearing: $20 \text{ Hz} \text{ to } 20 \text{ kHz}$; Telephone speech band: $300 \text{ Hz} \text{ to } 3400 \text{ Hz}$ ($8 \text{ kHz}$ sampling).
5. List the main components of a general speech coder.
Encoder (Filter/Sample → Analysis → Quantize), Bitstream Channel, and Decoder (Decode → Synthesis → Output).
6. Classify speech coding techniques into three major categories.
Waveform Coders (PCM/ADPCM), Parametric Coders / Vocoders (LPC-10), and Hybrid Coders (CELP family).
7. What is Mean Opinion Score (MOS)?
Subjective perceptual speech quality rating scale from 1 (unacceptable) to 5 (excellent). Toll quality phone calls require MOS ≥ 4.0.
8. Why is low coding delay critical in interactive speech communication?
Delays > 150 ms create annoying echo and disrupt natural two-way conversational interactions.
9. Define pitch period and fundamental frequency ($F_0$).
Pitch period $T_0$ is the time interval of one vocal fold vibration cycle; fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$.
10. How is pitch period estimated using autocorrelation?
By searching for the major secondary peak location at lag $k = P_0$ in the autocorrelation function $R[k]$.
11. Differentiate All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) filters.
All-pole filters $H(z) = 1/A(z)$ model vocal tract resonances (formants); all-zero filters $H(z) = B(z)$ model feedforward zeroes.
12. State the discrete-time convolution formula.
$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] h[n-k]$; combines input $x[n]$ with filter impulse response $h[n]$.
13. What is the Wiener-Khinchin theorem?
States that the Power Spectral Density (PSD) of a stationary signal is the Fourier Transform of its autocorrelation sequence.
14. Differentiate biased and unbiased autocorrelation estimators.
Biased divides by frame size $N$ (guarantees positive semi-definite PSD); unbiased divides by $N-k$.
