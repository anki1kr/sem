2.1 Basic Concepts of Linear Prediction & Normal Equations

Linear prediction estimates the current speech sample as a weighted linear combination of past speech samples. (purane samples se naya sample predict karo)

Definition: Linear prediction (LP) models the current speech sample $x[n]$ as a linear combination of $p$ previous samples: $$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$$ The prediction error is $$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n] = x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$$
  • Linear prediction kya hai: a signal processing technique that estimates the current (or a future) sample as a linear combination of past samples. Speech, audio and biomedical signals have correlated consecutive samples, so past values carry information about the next one. (past samples dekhkar present sample ka approximate value nikalna)
  • Core idea (expanded form): $$\hat{x}[n] = a_1x[n-1] + a_2x[n-2] + \dots + a_px[n-p]$$ where $x[n]$ = actual (current) sample, $\hat{x}[n]$ = predicted (estimated) sample, $x[n-1], x[n-2], \dots, x[n-p]$ = the $p$ previous samples, $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_p$ = prediction coefficients, $p$ = prediction order. (past samples × coefficients = predicted sample)
  • Prediction order $p$: tells how many previous samples are used. Example: $p = 3$ gives $\hat{x}[n] = a_1x[n-1] + a_2x[n-2] + a_3x[n-3]$. (p matlab past samples ki quantity)
  • Prediction error / residual: the difference between the actual and the predicted value, $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$. (jo mila minus jo socha = error)
  • Good predictor: if $\hat{x}[n] \approx x[n]$ then $e[n] \approx 0$. Good predictor = small error; its residual is close to white noise.
  • Why Speech is Predictable: Vocal tract inertia creates strong correlation between adjacent samples, and glottal quasi-periodicity creates inter-pitch correlation.
  • Derivation of Normal Equations: Choose $a_1, \dots, a_p$ so that the Mean Squared Prediction Error is minimum: $$E = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\Big(x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]\Big)^2$$ Setting $\partial E / \partial a_i = 0$ for each coefficient yields the Yule-Walker / Autocorrelation Normal Equations: $$\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,R(i-k) = R(i), \quad i = 1, \dots, p$$ where $R(i)$ is the autocorrelation function of $x[n]$ over a frame of $N$ samples: $$R(i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-i} x[n]\,x[n+i]$$ and $R(-i) = R(i)$, so $R(i-k) = R(|i-k|)$.
  • Matrix Form ($p=2$): $$\begin{bmatrix} R(0) & R(1) \\ R(1) & R(0) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R(1) \\ R(2) \end{bmatrix}$$

2.2 LP Analysis of Non-Stationary Signals & Prediction Gain

Speech is a non-stationary signal, so LP analysis is performed on short quasi-stationary frames of 20 to 30 milliseconds. (20-30 ms ke chhote frames me analysis hota hai)

Definition: Linear prediction analysis treats non-stationary speech as quasi-stationary by segmenting it into short overlapping frames (20 ms - 30 ms) multiplied by a smooth window function (such as a Hamming window).
  • Non-stationarity problem: standard LP theory assumes a stationary signal, i.e. its statistics (autocorrelation, spectral envelope) do not change over time. Real speech is non-stationary: the vocal-tract shape, pitch and energy change continuously as a person talks, so one set of $a_k$ cannot fit a whole utterance.
  • Solution: short-time (frame-based) analysis: speech changes slowly compared with the sampling rate, so it is treated as quasi-stationary over short intervals (typically 20-30 ms). Steps:
    1. Segment the signal into frames (often 50% overlap).
    2. Window each frame (Hamming / Hanning) to reduce edge discontinuities and spectral leakage.
    3. Analyze each frame independently and compute fresh LP coefficients $a_k(m)$.
  • Frame equation: $$x_m[n] = x[n]\,w[n - mL]$$ where $w[n]$ = window, $m$ = frame index, $L$ = frame shift (hop size). Example: a 20 ms frame at 8 kHz has 160 samples; 50% overlap gives $L = 80$ samples.
  • Result: time-varying LP coefficients $a_k(m)$, i.e. a new all-pole model per frame, which tracks the changing vocal tract / spectral envelope over time.
  • Autocorrelation Method vs Covariance Method:
    • Autocorrelation Method: Windows the speech frame so it vanishes outside bounds. Guarantees a stable filter ($|k_m| \lt 1$).
    • Covariance Method: Does not window signal outside frame; requires no zero-padding, but filter stability is not guaranteed.

  • Prediction Gain (PG): Quantitative measure of LP compression efficiency defined as the ratio of speech variance to prediction error variance: $$G_p = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}\right) = 10\log_{10}\frac{R(0)}{E_p} \ \text{dB}$$ where $\sigma_x^2$ = variance (power) of the original signal $x[n]$ and $\sigma_e^2$ = variance (power) of the residual $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$. It tells how much the predictor reduces the signal's energy, i.e. how much redundancy it removes.
  • Interpretation: large $G_p$ → predictor removes most of the redundancy, residual is small and close to white noise → effective model. Small $G_p$ → poor prediction, more residual energy (less compression / poor modelling), e.g. rapid signal changes or an unsuitable order.
  • Voiced vs unvoiced frames: voiced frames (vowels: periodic, strong formants) give a higher gain; unvoiced frames (fricatives: noise-like) give a lower gain.
  • Segmental prediction gain (SPG): in non-stationary signals $G_p$ varies from frame to frame, so the per-frame dB values are averaged over $M$ frames: $$SPG = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} G_p(m)$$
  • Relation to prediction order: increasing $p$ generally increases the gain (more detail captured), but after a point the gain saturates (extra coefficients only model noise / quantization). Typical order for speech: $p \approx 10$–$16$ for 8-16 kHz sampling, roughly 2 coefficients per formant plus a few extra.
  • Quality metric: per-frame gain is monitored to detect frames where the model fits poorly (e.g. onset / transient frames or transmission errors), which may need special handling (smaller frames, transition frames or different quantization). Practical use: speech / audio coding (CELP, ADPCM, FLAC), adaptive LP code design, speech enhancement and analysis.

2.3 Levinson-Durbin Algorithm & Reflection Coefficients

The Levinson-Durbin algorithm solves normal equations recursively in $O(p^2)$ time while generating reflection coefficients. (O(p^2) me fast solve aur stability test)

Definition: The Levinson-Durbin algorithm is an efficient recursive algorithm that solves the Yule-Walker matrix equations in $O(p^2)$ operations, computing predictor coefficients $a_k$ alongside intermediate reflection coefficients $k_m$. It uses the symmetric (Toeplitz) structure of the autocorrelation matrix instead of inverting it.
  • Problem it solves: given $R(0), R(1), \dots, R(p)$ of a frame, find $a_1, \dots, a_p$ such that $$\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,R(i-k) = R(i), \quad i = 1, \dots, p$$ without inverting a matrix. The coefficients are built order by order ($m = 1, 2, \dots, p$).
  • Toeplitz matrix: the Yule-Walker (autocorrelation) matrix is symmetric and every diagonal holds one constant value ($R(0)$ on the main diagonal, $R(1)$ on the next, and so on). Levinson-Durbin exploits this symmetry.
  • Recursion formulas (index $m$ = current order): $$E_0 = R(0)$$ For $m = 1, 2, \dots, p$: $$k_m = \frac{R(m) - \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} a_j^{(m-1)}\,R(m-j)}{E_{m-1}}$$ $$a_m^{(m)} = k_m, \qquad a_j^{(m)} = a_j^{(m-1)} - k_m\,a_{m-j}^{(m-1)} \quad (1 \le j \le m-1)$$ $$E_m = (1 - k_m^2)\,E_{m-1}$$ Final coefficients: $a_k = a_k^{(p)}$.
    Easy reading: new $a_j$ = old $a_j$ − $k_m$ × old $a_{m-j}$ (the mirror coefficient).
  • Reflection Coefficients ($k_m$): Intermediate parameters generated during Levinson-Durbin recursion (also called PARCOR, partial correlation, coefficients).
  • Stability Condition: An all-pole filter is strictly stable if and only if all reflection coefficients satisfy $|k_m| \lt 1$ for $m = 1, \dots, p$.
  • Computational Complexity: Reduces matrix inversion complexity from $O(p^3)$ to $O(p^2)$. Space complexity is $O(p)$ (only the coefficients and a few intermediate values are stored).
  • Advantages: fast and efficient; gives prediction coefficients, reflection coefficients and error at every order; exploits the symmetry of the autocorrelation matrix; widely used in speech coding, LPC and spectral analysis.

  4. Output: $a_k^{(p)}$ ($k = 1, \dots, p$), reflection coefficients $k_1, \dots, k_p$ and error $E_p$.

Worked Example: Levinson-Durbin Recursion Step-by-Step
Given $R(0) = 5.0$, $R(1) = 3.0$, $R(2) = 1.0$. Solve for order $p=2$.
Initialization: $E_0 = R(0) = 5.0$.
Iteration 1 ($m=1$):
$k_1 = \dfrac{R(1)}{E_0} = \dfrac{3.0}{5.0} = 0.6$ (Since $|0.6| \lt 1$, stable)
$a_1^{(1)} = k_1 = 0.6$
$E_1 = (1 - k_1^2)\,E_0 = (1 - 0.36) \times 5.0 = 0.64 \times 5.0 = 3.2$
Iteration 2 ($m=2$):
$k_2 = \dfrac{R(2) - a_1^{(1)} R(1)}{E_1} = \dfrac{1.0 - (0.6)(3.0)}{3.2} = \dfrac{1.0 - 1.8}{3.2} = \dfrac{-0.8}{3.2} = -0.25$ (Since $|-0.25| \lt 1$, stable)
$a_2^{(2)} = k_2 = -0.25$
$a_1^{(2)} = a_1^{(1)} - k_2\, a_1^{(1)} = 0.6 - (-0.25)(0.6) = 0.6 + 0.15 = 0.75$ (here $m = 2$, $j = 1$, so the mirror coefficient $a_{m-j}^{(1)} = a_1^{(1)}$)
$E_2 = (1 - k_2^2)\,E_1 = (1 - (-0.25)^2) \times 3.2 = (1 - 0.0625) \times 3.2 = 0.9375 \times 3.2 = 3.0$

2.4 Short-Term Linear Prediction (LPC) & Short-Term vs Long-Term Models

Short-term prediction removes vocal tract spectral envelope correlation, while long-term prediction removes pitch periodicity correlation. (short-term formant nikalta hai, long-term pitch)

Definition: A complete speech prediction system uses a cascade of a short-term predictor (order $p \approx 10$ modeling vocal tract formants) and a long-term predictor (pitch predictor modeling pitch period $T$).
  • Meaning of short-term LP: the current sample is predicted from a few previous (nearby) samples. It works because the speech signal changes smoothly over a short time. (nearby samples se predict kiya ja sakta hai)
  • Basic mechanism: $\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$, where $x[n]$ = current sample, $x[n-k]$ = previous samples, $a_k$ = LPC (prediction) coefficients, $p$ = order (typically 10-16).
  • LPC coefficients: $a_k$ represent the spectral (formant) characteristics; they act like a filter that models the shape of the vocal tract.
  • Physical meaning: short-term LP models the vocal-tract resonances (formants = resonant frequencies, the unique character of a sound). The vocal-tract filter is approximated by an all-pole model: only poles, no zeros. $$H(z) = \frac{G}{A(z)}, \qquad A(z) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k z^{-k}$$
  • Residual: $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$ captures the remaining part that could not be predicted (the excitation).
  • Why re-analysed per frame: speech changes with time (different sounds, syllables, pauses), so one set of coefficients cannot be used for the whole signal.
  • Short frames, overlap, windowing: frame length 20-30 ms ($\approx$ 160-240 samples at 8 kHz); frames overlap (e.g. a 10 ms shift gives $L = 80$ samples at 8 kHz); a Hamming / Hanning window reduces edge discontinuities; separate LPC coefficients $a_k(m)$ are calculated for every frame, which gives smooth transitions and better spectral analysis.
  • How the coefficients are found: minimise the MSE using the Yule-Walker equations $\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k R(i-k) = R(i)$ with $R(i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-i} x[n]\,x[n+i]$.

2.5 Long-Term Prediction (LTP): Pitch Lag, Gain & 3-Tap LTP

Long-term prediction removes the pitch repetition that short-term LPC leaves behind in voiced speech. (LPC formant hataata hai, LTP pitch ki repetition hataata hai)

Definition: Long-term prediction (LTP) predicts the short-term residual $e[n]$ from the residual one pitch period earlier: $$e[n] \approx \beta\, e[n-T]$$ where $T$ = pitch period (lag) in samples and $\beta$ = long-term prediction coefficient.
  • Purpose: short-term LPC ($p \approx 10$–$16$) models the vocal tract / formant structure using a few samples, but it does not capture the pitch periodicity of voiced speech. LTP uses past samples from a longer time back (one pitch period) to predict the current sample.
  • Model: instead of predicting $x[n]$ directly, LTP predicts the residual $e[n]$ from its delayed (pitch-period) version. The final long-term residual is $$r[n] = e[n] - \beta\, e[n-T]$$ and the LTP error filter in $z$-form is $$P(z) = 1 - \beta z^{-T}$$
  • Pitch lag range: pitch periods are about 2.5-20 ms ($F_0$ about 50-400 Hz), so at 8 kHz the lag $T$ is about 20-160 samples.
  • Key points: $r[n]$ is much flatter and lower in energy than $e[n]$, since both formant (vocal tract) and pitch structure are removed; this helps better modelling and coding of voiced speech.
  • Estimating $T$ and $\beta$: both are found by minimising the sum of squared error over a search range of candidate lags: $$\beta(T) = \frac{\sum_n e[n]\,e[n-T]}{\sum_n e^2[n-T]}, \qquad E(T) = \sum_n r^2[n]$$
    1. Choose the possible values (search range) of $T$.
    2. For each $T$, calculate $\beta(T)$.
    3. Compute the residual $r[n] = e[n] - \beta\, e[n-T]$.
    4. Calculate the error energy $E(T) = \sum r^2[n]$.
    5. Select the $T$ for which $E(T)$ is minimum: this is the pitch lag, and its $\beta(T)$ is the coefficient.
  • Higher-order (3-tap) LTP: single-tap LTP uses only one delayed sample and may be inaccurate when the pitch period is not a whole number of samples. So three nearby samples ($T-1$, $T$, $T+1$) are used: $$\hat{e}[n] = \beta_{-1}\, e[n-T-1] + \beta_0\, e[n-T] + \beta_1\, e[n-T+1]$$ Benefit: works better even when the pitch period is not exact.
  • Prediction gain in two stages: $$G_{p,\text{short}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}, \qquad G_{p,\text{long}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma_e^2}{\sigma_r^2}$$ $$G_{p,\text{total}} = G_{p,\text{short}} + G_{p,\text{long}} \ \text{dB}$$ where $\sigma_x^2$ = original signal power, $\sigma_e^2$ = power after LPC (residual), $\sigma_r^2$ = power after LTP (final residual).

2.6 Moving Average (MA) Prediction Models: AR vs MA vs ARMA

An MA model builds each sample only from present and past inputs, with no feedback, so it can create dips (zeros) but not sharp peaks. (MA = past inputs ka mixture, koi feedback nahi)

Definition: A moving average (MA) model of order $q$ forms the signal as a weighted sum of the current and $q$ past input (excitation) samples: $$x[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{q} b_k\, u[n-k]$$ In MA prediction the unknown past inputs are replaced by the past prediction errors: $$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{q} b_k\, e[n-k]$$
ModelPredicts fromFilter type
AR (autoregressive)Past output samples $x[n-k]$ (short-term LPC)All-pole
MA (moving average)Past input / excitation samples $u[n-k]$ (in prediction: past errors $e[n-k]$)All-zero
ARMABoth (past output + input)Pole-zero
  • Context: the short-term LPC studied so far is an AR model ($p$ past samples). MA uses past prediction errors instead of past output samples; ARMA is the combination of both.
  • MA model terms: $u[n]$ = input / excitation sequence (white noise, etc.), $b_k$ = MA coefficients ($k = 0, 1, \dots, q$), $q$ = order of MA (number of past inputs used).
  • No feedback & FIR: only past input samples are used, so the model is non-recursive. Its impulse response is finite, i.e. FIR (Finite Impulse Response): $$h[n] = b_n \ \text{for}\ n = 0, 1, \dots, q, \qquad h[n] = 0 \ \text{after that}$$ Length of the impulse response $= q + 1$ nonzero terms. (FIR = finite length)
  • AR(1) model: $x[n] = a_1 x[n-1] + u[n]$ with $u[n]$ = white noise; impulse response $h[n] = a_1^{\,n}$ for $n \ge 0$, infinite and decaying if $|a_1| \lt 1$.
  • MA(1) model: $x[n] = u[n] + b_1 u[n-1]$; impulse response $h[n] = \{1, b_1, 0, 0, \dots\}$, finite (stops after $n = 1$).

  • Why AR is preferred for speech:
    • Vocal-tract resonances (formants) are sharp spectral peaks, and AR (poles) captures peaks efficiently.
    • All-pole model = few coefficients, less computation, linear Yule-Walker equations.
    • An MA model would need a very high order to produce the same sharp peaks.
    • So AR is used alone, or together with MA as a combined ARMA model.
  • Where MA structure actually shows up:
    • Nasal sounds (/m/, /n/): coupling of the nasal cavity adds anti-resonances (zeros); a pure AR model cannot represent them well and needs extra poles.
    • ARMA models: AR part = peaks, MA part = zeros.
    • In coding: MA-type (FIR) filters appear, e.g. the pre-emphasis filter $1 - \alpha z^{-1}$ (its inverse, de-emphasis, is all-pole).

MA(1): $x[n] = u[n] + 0.8\,u[n-1]$, so $h[0] = 1$, $h[1] = 0.8$, $h[2] = h[3] = \dots = 0$ (length $q + 1 = 2$).
Stability: AR(1) is stable only because $|a_1| = 0.8 \lt 1$ (pole at $z = 0.8$ inside the unit circle). MA(1) is stable for any $b_1$; even $b_1 = 5$ gives just $\{1, 5, 0, 0, \dots\}$.
For any $b_1$ the MA(1) response has only two taps, so it never produces a sharp spectral peak: the "ringing" is small.

Common mistake: (1) The AR(1) impulse response is $h[n] = a_1^{\,n}$ for $n \ge 0$; writing $h[n] = a_1^n\,h[n]$ is a slip. (2) MA prediction uses past errors $e[n-k]$ (inputs), not past samples $x[n-k]$; past samples would make it AR. (3) Pre-emphasis $1 - \alpha z^{-1}$ is FIR, but de-emphasis is all-pole, so it is not an FIR (MA) filter.
Quick recall AR: past outputs, poles, IIR, peaks (formants), needs a stability check. MA: past inputs / errors, zeros, FIR with $h[n] = b_n$ ($q+1$ terms), always stable. ARMA: both, more flexible, needs a stability check.

2.7 Scalar Quantization (Uniform, Optimum Lloyd-Max, Logarithmic Companding)

Quantization maps continuous amplitude values into discrete levels, introducing irreversible quantization noise. (continuous amplitude ko fixed levels me convert karna)

Definition: Scalar quantization maps a continuous amplitude scalar sample $x$ to a discrete reconstruction level $y_k = Q(x)$ selected from a set of decision boundaries $d_k$.
  • Uniform Quantizer: Equal step size $$\Delta = \frac{x_{max} - x_{min}}{L}$$
    • Mid-tread: Origin 0 is a reconstruction level.
    • Mid-rise: Origin 0 is a decision boundary.
    • SQNR Formula (for $B$-bit quantizer with full-scale sinusoid): $$SQNR \approx 6.02B + 1.76 \ \text{dB}$$ Rule of thumb: Each additional bit adds $\approx 6$ dB of SQNR.
  • Optimum Lloyd-Max Quantizer: Non-uniform quantizer that minimizes Mean Squared Error (MSE) for a known PDF:
    • Boundary Condition: Decision boundary (midpoint of adjacent levels): $$d_k = \frac{y_k + y_{k+1}}{2}$$
    • Reconstruction Condition: $y_k$ = centroid of its region (PDF interval): $$y_k = \frac{\int_{d_{k-1}}^{d_k} x\, p(x)\, dx}{\int_{d_{k-1}}^{d_k} p(x)\, dx}$$
  • Logarithmic Quantizer (Companding): Compresses dynamic range before uniform quantization and expands at receiver:
    • μ-law (North America/Japan): with $\mu = 255$, $$C(x) = \frac{\ln(1+\mu|x|)}{\ln(1+\mu)}\,\text{sgn}(x)$$
    • A-law (Europe/International): Uses $A = 87.6$.
  • Role in speech coding: quantization sets the bit rate needed to store or send speech. Fewer bits give a lower rate but more distortion (bit rate vs quality trade-off); in LPC coders it is the LP coefficients and the residual that get quantized.
Worked Example: Uniform Quantizer SQNR and Companding Calculation
1. 3-Bit Uniform Quantizer: $B = 3$ bits ($L = 2^3 = 8$ levels).
$SQNR \approx 6.02(3) + 1.76 = 18.06 + 1.76 = 19.82$ dB.
2. Logarithmic μ-law compression for $x = 0.5$ ($\mu = 255$):
$C(0.5) = \dfrac{\ln(1 + 255 \times 0.5)}{\ln(1 + 255)} = \dfrac{\ln(128.5)}{\ln(256)} = \dfrac{4.8559}{5.5452} = 0.8757$.
$SQNR = 19.82$ dB (3-bit); Compressed mu-law value $C(0.5) = 0.8757$
Quick recall Uniform $SQNR \approx 6.02B + 1.76$ dB; Lloyd-Max places boundaries midway and levels at centroids; μ-law ($\mu = 255$) compresses weak signals.

2.8 Adaptive and Differential Quantizers (AQF, AQB, DPCM, ADPCM)

Adaptive quantizers adjust step sizes dynamically, while differential quantizers quantize the difference between actual and predicted samples. (step size change karo aur difference ko quantize karo)

Definition: Adaptive quantization adapts quantizer step size $\Delta[n]$ to local signal variance, while differential quantization (DPCM) quantizes difference samples $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$ to exploit sample correlation.
  • Adaptive Quantization:
    • Forward AQF: Step size calculated from input speech frame; transmitted as side information.
    • Backward AQB: Step size estimated from past quantized output; no side information needed.
  • Differential PCM (DPCM): Encoder quantizes the difference $$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$$ Predictor lives in feedback loop using reconstructed samples $\tilde{x}[n]$ so encoder and decoder stay synchronized: $$\tilde{x}[n] = \hat{x}[n] + \hat{e}[n]$$ where $\hat{e}[n]$ is the quantized difference.
  • ADPCM (Adaptive DPCM): Combines adaptive quantization step size with an adaptive predictor (e.g., ITU G.726 @ 32 kbps).
Quick recall AQF transmits explicit step size; AQB estimates it from past quantized output. DPCM quantizes difference $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$ and rebuilds $\tilde{x}[n] = \hat{x}[n] + \hat{e}[n]$; ADPCM adds adaptive prediction.

2.9 Vector Quantization (Distortion Measures, LBG Algorithm, Codebook Types)

Vector quantization groups vectors of samples and matches them to the nearest codeword in a stored codebook. (poore vector ko codebook ke codeword se match karo)

Definition: Vector quantization (VQ) blocks $k$ consecutive samples into a $k$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x}$ and maps it to the closest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$ in a codebook $C = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_N\}$, transmitting only the codebook index $i$.
  • Distortion Measures:
    • Squared Euclidean Distance (MSE): $$d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 = \sum (x_i - y_i)^2$$
    • Weighted Euclidean Distance: $$d = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{W} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})$$
    • Itakura-Saito Distance: Measures spectral distortion between LPC spectral envelopes $P(\omega)$ and $\hat{P}(\omega)$.
  • LBG Algorithm (Linde-Buzo-Gray / Generalized Lloyd Algorithm):
    1. Start with initial codebook (or split overall centroid).
    2. Partitioning: Assign training vectors to nearest codevector using distortion measure (Voronoi region partition).
    3. Update: Recompute each codevector as the centroid of assigned vectors.
    4. Check Convergence: If the fractional distortion reduction is small, stop; else repeat: $$\frac{D_{old} - D_{new}}{D_{old}} \lt \epsilon$$

2.10 Short answers (1 mark)

1. Define Linear Prediction in speech processing.
Estimation technique that predicts current speech sample $x[n]$ as a weighted sum of $p$ past samples: $\hat{x}[n] = \sum a_k x[n-k]$.
2. What are the Yule-Walker normal equations?
Set of matrix equations $\sum a_k R(i-k) = R(i)$ derived by minimizing mean squared prediction error.
3. Why is speech analyzed in short frames?
Because speech is non-stationary over long durations but quasi-stationary over short 20-30 ms windows.
4. Define Prediction Gain (PG).
Ratio of speech signal variance to prediction error variance: $G_p = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2)$ dB.
5. State the computational complexity of the Levinson-Durbin algorithm.
Solves normal equations recursively in $O(p^2)$ operations compared to $O(p^3)$ for direct matrix inversion.
6. What condition on reflection coefficients ($k_m$) guarantees filter stability?
The all-pole synthesis filter is stable if and only if $|k_m| \lt 1$ for all $m = 1, \dots, p$.
7. Differentiate short-term and long-term linear prediction.
Short-term LP ($p \approx 10$) models vocal tract formants; long-term LP models pitch periodicity $T$.
8. What is Moving Average (MA) prediction?
Predicts current sample from past prediction errors rather than past speech samples.
9. State the SQNR formula for a $B$-bit uniform quantizer.
$SQNR \approx 6.02B + 1.76$ dB for a full-scale sinusoidal signal.
10. State the two Lloyd-Max optimum quantization conditions.
(1) Decision boundary is midpoint of adjacent levels $d_k = (y_k + y_{k+1})/2$; (2) Level $y_k$ is the centroid of the interval PDF.
11. What is logarithmic companding?
Non-uniform quantization technique passing signal through a logarithmic compressor (μ-law / A-law) before uniform quantization.
12. Differentiate forward (AQF) and backward (AQB) adaptive quantization.
AQF calculates step size from input frame and transmits it; AQB estimates step size from past quantized output.
13. Explain the basic principle of Differential PCM (DPCM).
Quantizes the difference $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$ between current sample and predicted sample.
14. What is Vector Quantization (VQ)?
Quantization technique mapping a $k$-dimensional vector of samples to the nearest codevector codeword in a codebook.
15. What is the LBG algorithm?
Iterative codebook design algorithm (Generalized Lloyd) alternating between Voronoi partitioning and centroid updating.
16. What is prediction order $p$?
The number of past samples used by the predictor; e.g. $p = 3$ uses $x[n-1]$, $x[n-2]$, $x[n-3]$. Speech LPC typically uses $p \approx 10$–$16$.
17. Define the prediction residual with an example.
$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$, the part the predictor misses. If $x[n] = 20$ and $\hat{x}[n] = 18$, then $e[n] = 2$. A good predictor keeps $e[n] \approx 0$.
18. Write the short-time frame equation and explain its symbols.
$x_m[n] = x[n]\,w[n-mL]$, where $w[n]$ = window (Hamming / Hanning), $m$ = frame index and $L$ = frame shift (hop size). Frames are 20-30 ms with about 50% overlap.
19. What is segmental prediction gain (SPG)?
The average of the per-frame prediction gains in dB over $M$ frames: $SPG = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} G_p(m)$. It is used because $G_p$ changes from frame to frame in non-stationary speech.
20. Why do voiced frames give a higher prediction gain than unvoiced frames?
Voiced frames are periodic and strongly correlated, so the residual is small; unvoiced frames are noise-like with weak correlation (e.g. Frame A: $6.06$ dB, Frame B: $0.32$ dB).
21. What is a Toeplitz matrix? Why does Levinson-Durbin use it?
A symmetric matrix whose every diagonal holds one constant value. The Yule-Walker autocorrelation matrix is Toeplitz, which lets Levinson-Durbin solve it recursively in $O(p^2)$.
22. Write the long-term prediction (LTP) model.
$e[n] \approx \beta\, e[n-T]$ with final residual $r[n] = e[n] - \beta\, e[n-T]$, where $T$ = pitch lag and $\beta$ = LTP coefficient; $P(z) = 1 - \beta z^{-T}$.
23. How are the pitch lag $T$ and coefficient $\beta$ estimated?
For each candidate $T$, compute $\beta(T) = \sum e[n]e[n-T] / \sum e^2[n-T]$ and the error $E(T) = \sum r^2[n]$; choose the $T$ with minimum $E(T)$.
24. What is 3-tap LTP and why is it used?
$\hat{e}[n] = \beta_{-1}e[n-T-1] + \beta_0 e[n-T] + \beta_1 e[n-T+1]$. It works better when the pitch period is not a whole number of samples.
25. What is the typical pitch lag range at 8 kHz sampling?
Pitch periods are about 2.5-20 ms ($F_0$ about 50-400 Hz), i.e. a lag of about 20-160 samples.
26. Give the total prediction gain of a two-stage (LPC + LTP) predictor.
$G_{p,\text{short}} + G_{p,\text{long}} = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2) + 10\log_{10}(\sigma_e^2/\sigma_r^2)$ dB.
27. Differentiate AR and MA models.
AR predicts from past outputs, is all-pole (IIR) and models sharp peaks; MA uses past inputs / errors, is all-zero (FIR) and models dips (anti-resonances).
28. Why is an MA model always stable?
It has no feedback: its impulse response $h[n] = b_n$ for $n = 0, \dots, q$ and zero after, so the output can never grow without limit.
29. Why is AR preferred over MA for speech?
Formants are sharp spectral peaks that a few poles capture; AR coefficients come from linear Yule-Walker equations, while MA would need a very high order.
30. Expand ARMA and state where MA structure appears in speech.
AutoRegressive Moving Average (poles + zeros). MA structure appears in nasal sounds (anti-resonances / zeros) and in FIR filters such as pre-emphasis.
