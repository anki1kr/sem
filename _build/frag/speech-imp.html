Q1. Explain the human speech production mechanism and the Source-Filter model with a neat diagram.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: The three physiological production stages, anatomical components table, voiced vs. unvoiced speech contrast, and the Source-Filter block diagram.

Definition: Speech production is the physiological process where air expelled from the lungs passes through the vocal folds (vocal cords in the larynx) to create an acoustic excitation source, which is subsequently filtered and shaped by the resonant vocal tract (pharynx, oral, and nasal cavities) to produce intelligible speech. The Source-Filter Model represents speech $s[n]$ mathematically as the convolution of an acoustic excitation source $e[n]$ with a linear time-varying vocal tract filter $H(z)$.
  • The Three Stages of Speech Production:
    1. Conceptualization (Brain): The speaker conceives an idea, intention, or thought to communicate.
    2. Formulation (Linguistic): The brain converts the concept into linguistic structure—selecting words, grammar, syntax, and phonetic sequences.
    3. Articulation (Physiological): Motor nerves command the lungs, vocal folds, tongue, jaw, and lips to physically push air and shape sound pressure waves.
  • Key Anatomical Organs:
    • Lungs: The power supply / pump that expels air pressure through the trachea.
    • Larynx & Vocal Folds: Located in the throat. The vocal folds act like vibrating reeds or guitar strings to generate sound pulses.
    • Vocal Tract: The acoustic resonating tube (~17 cm long in adults) extending from the vocal cords to the lips and nostrils, consisting of the pharynx, oral cavity, and nasal cavity.
    • Articulators: Moving parts (tongue, lips, lower jaw, velum/soft palate) that continuously change the cross-sectional shape of the vocal tract, altering its resonance frequencies (formants).
  • Voiced vs. Unvoiced Speech Sounds:
    • Voiced Sounds: The vocal folds are held taut under tension. Air pressure forces them to rapidly snap open and shut in a quasi-periodic cycle. This creates a buzzing periodic impulse train with a distinct pitch period $T_0$. Examples: All vowels (/a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/) and nasals (/m/, /n/).
    • Unvoiced Sounds: The vocal folds remain relaxed and wide open. Air rushes through narrow constrictions in the mouth (e.g. between tongue and teeth), generating turbulent, random white noise with zero vocal cord vibration (no pitch). Examples: Fricatives and stops (/s/, /f/, /t/, /sh/).
  • The Source-Filter Equation: Mathematically, speech is generated by scaling the excitation source by gain $G$ and passing it through the vocal tract filter $H(z)$: $$S(z) = E(z) \cdot G \cdot H(z)$$ In Linear Predictive Coding (LPC), the vocal tract is modeled as an all-pole filter: $$H(z) = \frac{1}{A(z)} = \frac{1}{1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k z^{-k}}$$

Q2. Describe the human auditory system: parts of the ear, the pathway to the brain, and how hearing works.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: Three-part anatomical structure (Outer, Middle, Inner ear), acoustic impedance matching, cochlear tonotopic mapping, and neural pathway to the auditory cortex.

Definition: The Human Auditory System is the biological sensory mechanism that captures acoustic sound pressure waves, converts them into mechanical vibrations, decomposes them into component frequencies along the basilar membrane of the cochlea, and transmits neural electrical impulses along the auditory nerve to the brain's auditory cortex.
  • Key Frequency Benchmarks:
    • Human Audible Frequency Range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz), with maximum sensitivity between 1 kHz and 4 kHz (where human speech formants reside).
    • Standard Telephone Bandwidth: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (sampled at 8 kHz in PSTN telecom).
  • 1. Outer Ear (Acoustic Capture): Consists of the visible external ear (pinna) and the ear canal. The pinna acts like a satellite dish, gathering sound pressure waves from the environment and funneling them down the ear canal to strike the tympanic membrane (eardrum), causing it to vibrate.
  • 2. Middle Ear (Mechanical Impedance Matching): An air-filled cavity containing the three smallest bones in the human body, known as the ossicles (Mnemonic MIS):
    1. Malleus (Hammer): Attached directly to the eardrum.
    2. Incus (Anvil): Connected as an intermediate lever.
    3. Stapes (Stirrup): Pushes against the fluid-filled oval window of the inner ear.
    Why are ossicles critical? Air has very low acoustic impedance, while the fluid inside the inner ear has high impedance. If sound waves hit fluid directly, over 99.9% of sound energy would bounce right off. The ossicles act as a mechanical lever system, amplifying pressure by roughly 22:1 to drive the cochlear fluid efficiently.
  • 3. Inner Ear & Cochlea (Biological Frequency Analyzer): The cochlea is a snail-shaped, fluid-filled spiral structure containing the basilar membrane and the Organ of Corti.
    Tonotopic Frequency Mapping: The basilar membrane acts like an unrolled piano keyboard:
    • Base (near oval window): Narrow, stiff, and taut → resonates to high frequencies (up to 20 kHz).
    • Apex (inner tip): Wide, flexible, and floppy → resonates to low frequencies (down to 20 Hz).
    Thousands of microscopic hair cells on the membrane bend with fluid movement, converting physical vibrations into electrical nerve impulses.
  • The 6-Step Neural Pathway to the Brain: Hair cells → Auditory nerve → Cochlear Nucleus → Superior Olivary Complex (binaural sound localization) → Lateral Lemniscus → Inferior Colliculus → Medial Geniculate Body → Auditory Cortex in the temporal lobe of the brain.

Q3. Explain the general structure of a speech coder and classify speech coding techniques (Waveform, Parametric, Hybrid) with examples.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: Encoder-decoder system architecture, classification tree, comparison of the three codec families (Waveform vs Parametric vs Hybrid), and a compression-ratio worked example.

Definition: A speech coder (codec) is a digital communication system consisting of an encoder that analyzes and compresses raw speech into a compact bitstream, and a decoder that reconstructs synthesized speech from that bitstream at the receiving end. Speech coding techniques are classified into three major families: Waveform Coders, Parametric Coders (Vocoders), and Hybrid Coders.
  • 1. Waveform Coders (Wave-Preserving): Aims to preserve the exact physical shape of the analog speech waveform sample by sample without relying on any speech production model.
    Examples: Standard PCM (ITU-T G.711 @ 64 kbps), DPCM, ADPCM (G.726 @ 32 kbps).
    Trade-off: High speech quality (MOS 4.0–4.5) and robust to background noise, but requires high bit rates (16 to 64 kbps).
  • 2. Parametric Coders (Vocoders / Model-Based): Discards the waveform entirely! Assumes the Source-Filter speech model and transmits only mathematical model parameters: pitch period $T_0$, voiced/unvoiced flag, gain $G$, and all-pole vocal tract filter coefficients ($a_1, \dots, a_p$).
    Examples: LPC-10 (FS-1015 @ 2.4 kbps), MELP (2.4 kbps military radio).
    Trade-off: Extremely low bit rate (1.2 to 2.4 kbps), but produces synthetic, mechanical, "robotic" sounding speech with poor speaker identification.
  • 3. Hybrid Coders (Analysis-by-Synthesis): The modern industry standard that combines the high quality of waveform coders with the low bandwidth of vocoders. It models the vocal tract using LPC, but optimizes the excitation waveform using an indexed codebook searching for the minimum perceptual error.
    Examples: CELP (Code-Excited Linear Prediction), ACELP (ITU-T G.729 @ 8 kbps, AMR in 3G/4G VoLTE).
    Trade-off: Achieves commercial "toll quality" (MOS ~4.0) at modest bit rates (4.8 to 16 kbps).

Q4. Discuss the requirements of speech codecs: quality, coding delay, and robustness, with trade-offs.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: The four fundamental design constraints (Bit Rate, Quality, Delay, Robustness), ITU-T G.114 latency boundaries, and an SNR calculation.

Definition: The engineering performance of any speech codec is governed by a multi-dimensional optimization problem balancing four conflicting requirements: Bit Rate (minimizing transmission bandwidth), Perceptual Quality (maximizing intelligibility and naturalness), Processing Delay (minimizing latency for smooth real-time conversation), and Robustness (resilience against channel noise, packet loss, and background chatter).
  • 1. Speech Quality: Evaluated across three criteria: Intelligibility (can words be identified?), Naturalness (does it sound like a human?), and Speaker Recognition.
    Metrics:
    • MOS (Mean Opinion Score): Subjective listening score from 1 (Bad) to 5 (Excellent). Commercial PSTN standard "toll quality" requires MOS ≥ 4.0.
    • PESQ (Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality): ITU-T standard objective algorithm predicting MOS scores automatically.
    • SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio): Objective mathematical ratio of signal energy to reconstruction error energy in decibels.
  • 2. Coding Delay (Latency): The time elapsed between a sound entering the microphone and exiting the receiver's speaker.
    Components: $$\text{Total Delay} = \text{Frame Size (10-30 ms)} + \text{Lookahead Delay} + \text{Processing Time} + \text{Network Transmission}$$ If one-way delay exceeds 150 ms, interactive conversation suffers from annoying talk-over collisions and acoustic echoes.
  • 3. Robustness: The codec's ability to maintain intelligible output under adverse channel conditions:
    • Packet Loss Concealment (PLC): Synthesizes plausible substitute frames when IP network packets are dropped.
    • VAD & CNG: Voice Activity Detection (VAD) halts transmission during pauses and silence (cutting bandwidth by ~50%). Comfort Noise Generation (CNG) plays faint synthesized background noise at the receiver so the caller does not think the line dropped!

Worked Numerical Example: Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) Calculation for a Speech Frame
Original speech frame samples: $x = [4, -2, 3, 1]$
Decoded speech frame samples: $\tilde{x} = [3.9, -2.1, 3.2, 0.9]$
Error vector: $e = x - \tilde{x} = [4 - 3.9, -2 - (-2.1), 3 - 3.2, 1 - 0.9] = [0.1, 0.1, -0.2, 0.1]$
Original signal power sum: $\sum x^2[n] = 4^2 + (-2)^2 + 3^2 + 1^2 = 16 + 4 + 9 + 1 = 30$
Reconstruction error power sum: $\sum e^2[n] = (0.1)^2 + (0.1)^2 + (-0.2)^2 + (0.1)^2 = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.07$
Calculate SNR in decibels: $$\text{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sum x^2[n]}{\sum e^2[n]}\right) = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{30}{0.07}\right) = 10\log_{10}(428.57) \approx 10 \times 2.632 = \mathbf{26.32\text{ dB}}$$
Result: Decoded frame has an SNR of 26.32 dB (indicates clear, high-quality reconstruction).
Mnemonic Codec Triangle: Q-D-B → Quality, Delay, Bit rate. You can optimize any two, but the third must be compromised!

Q5. Explain the methods of pitch-period estimation and compute the pitch period for a given lag using autocorrelation.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: Physical meaning of pitch, the autocorrelation curve with secondary peak, comparison of detection methods (Autocorrelation, AMDF, Cepstrum), and a full numerical calculation of $T_0$ and $F_0$.

Definition: Pitch-period estimation (pitch tracking) is the signal processing technique used to determine the fundamental period $T_0$ (in seconds) or fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$ (in Hz) at which the human vocal folds vibrate during voiced speech segments.
  • Human Fundamental Frequency Ranges:
    • Adult Men: 80 Hz to 160 Hz (average ~120 Hz; longer, thicker vocal cords).
    • Adult Women: 150 Hz to 300 Hz (average ~220 Hz; shorter, thinner vocal cords).
    • Children: 250 Hz to 500 Hz (average ~300+ Hz).
  • 1. Autocorrelation Method (Time Domain): Measures the mathematical similarity between a signal and a time-delayed copy of itself: $$R(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] \cdot x[n+k]$$ Intuition (Fig 1.7): At zero delay ($k=0$), the signal aligns with itself perfectly, producing maximum energy $R(0)$. For voiced speech, the wave repeats every pitch period. Therefore, as lag $k$ increases, $R(k)$ exhibits a prominent secondary peak at lag $k = P_0$. The pitch period in seconds is: $$T_0 = \frac{P_0}{f_s} \quad \text{and} \quad F_0 = \frac{f_s}{P_0}$$
  • 2. AMDF (Average Magnitude Difference Function): Replaces computationally heavy multiplications with simple subtractions: $$\text{AMDF}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} |x[n] - x[n+k]|$$ Instead of looking for a peak, the pitch period corresponds to a sharp minimum dip (valley) where the signal subtracted from itself approaches zero. Ideal for low-power DSP hardware!
  • 3. Cepstral Method (Quefrency Domain): Takes the inverse Fourier transform of the log-magnitude spectrum: $c[n] = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\log |X(\omega)|\}$. Because the log converts multiplication (Source $\times$ Filter) into addition, the slowly varying vocal tract envelope separates cleanly from the rapid excitation harmonics, leaving an isolated pitch peak at "quefrency" $T_0$.
  • 4. SIFT (Simplified Inverse Filter Tracking): Uses an inverse LPC filter to strip away vocal tract resonances first, leaving the raw glottal residual. Autocorrelating this clean residual yields an unambiguous pitch peak free of formant interference.

Worked Numerical Example: Calculating $T_0$ and $F_0$ from Autocorrelation Lag
Given: Speech sampled at $f_s = 8000\text{ Hz}$ (telephone quality). Autocorrelation analysis shows that the highest non-zero peak occurs at lag $P_0 = 80\text{ samples}$.
Calculate Pitch Period in seconds ($T_0$): $$T_0 = \frac{P_0}{f_s} = \frac{80}{8000} = 0.01\text{ s} = \mathbf{10\text{ ms}}$$
Calculate Fundamental Frequency ($F_0$): $$F_0 = \frac{1}{T_0} = \frac{1}{0.01} = \mathbf{100\text{ Hz}}$$
Conclusion: The speaker has a pitch period of 10 ms and a fundamental frequency of 100 Hz (identifies an adult male voice).

Q6. Define poles and zeros, compare all-pole and all-zero filters, and explain discrete-time convolution with a worked example.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: Transfer function poles and zeros, stability conditions, block diagrams for All-Pole (IIR) vs All-Zero (FIR) filters, and a complete step-by-step convolution calculation.

Definition: In a discrete-time linear system with rational transfer function $H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)}$, zeros are the roots of the numerator polynomial $B(z)$ where system gain drops to zero ($H(z) = 0$). Poles are the roots of the denominator polynomial $A(z)$ where system gain approaches infinity ($H(z) \to \infty$). An All-Pole filter has only poles (IIR feedback structure); an All-Zero filter has only zeros (FIR feedforward structure). Convolution combines an input signal $x[n]$ with an impulse response $h[n]$ to compute system output: $y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_k x[k] h[n-k]$.
  • All-Pole Filter (IIR — Infinite Impulse Response): Transfer function: $H(z) = \frac{G}{1 - \sum_{k=1}^p a_k z^{-k}}$.
    Feedback Architecture: Current output depends on current input plus weighted past outputs.
    Stability Rule: Strictly stable if and only if all poles lie strictly inside the unit circle ($|z_p| < 1$). If even one pole lies on or outside $|z| = 1$, the filter explodes into oscillation.
    Role in Speech: Models vocal tract formant resonances (peaks) in Linear Predictive Coding (LPC).
  • All-Zero Filter (FIR — Finite Impulse Response): Transfer function: $H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^M b_k z^{-k}$.
    Feedforward Architecture: Output depends solely on current and past inputs (no feedback loop).
    Stability Rule: Always inherently BIBO stable! All $M$ poles reside harmlessly at the origin ($z=0$), which is strictly inside $|z| < 1$.
    Role in Speech: Models anti-resonances, spectral nulls, and nasal consonants (/m/, /n/).
  • Discrete Convolution (The 4 Operational Steps) (Fig 1.9):
    1. Flip: Reflect impulse response $h[k]$ around origin to get $h[-k]$.
    2. Shift: Slide the flipped sequence by $n$ steps to obtain $h[n-k]$.
    3. Multiply: Multiply overlapping samples of $x[k]$ and $h[n-k]$.
    4. Sum: Add all product terms together to produce output sample $y[n]$.
    Total Output Length: If $x[n]$ has length $N$ and $h[n]$ has length $M$, output $y[n]$ has length $L = N + M - 1$.

Input signal: $x[n] = [1, 2, 3]$ (Length $N = 3$)
Impulse response: $h[n] = [2, 1]$ (Length $M = 2$)
Output length: $L = N + M - 1 = 3 + 2 - 1 = \mathbf{4\text{ samples}}$ ($y[0]$ to $y[3]$).
Compute each sample using $y[n] = \sum_k x[k] h[n-k]$:
• $n = 0$: $y[0] = x[0]h[0] = 1 \times 2 = \mathbf{2}$

Q7. Explain Power Spectral Density, the Periodogram, the Autoregressive model, and Autocorrelation estimation.

Unit I · 8 marks · Exam focus: The Wiener-Khinchin theorem, Periodogram spectral estimation and its variance limits, the Autoregressive (AR) parametric model, and Biased vs. Unbiased autocorrelation estimators with a worked numeric.

Definition: Power Spectral Density (PSD) $S_{xx}(\omega)$ describes how the statistical power of a random signal is distributed across frequency. According to the foundational Wiener-Khinchin Theorem, for any wide-sense stationary (WSS) discrete-time random process, the PSD is the Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT) of its autocorrelation sequence: $$S_{xx}(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} R_{xx}(k) e^{-j\omega k}$$
  • 1. The Periodogram (Non-Parametric Direct Estimator): Computes PSD directly by squaring the magnitude of the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of an $N$-sample windowed frame: $$\hat{S}_{\text{per}}(\omega) = \frac{1}{N} \left| \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-j\omega n} \right|^2$$ Major Drawbacks (Fig 1.10):
    • High Variance (Noisy Spectrum): The variance of the periodogram estimate does not decrease to zero even as data length $N \to \infty$. It produces a jagged, erratic curve that obscures smooth speech formants.
    • Spectral Leakage: Finite rectangular windowing causes energy from strong peaks to bleed into neighboring frequency bins (mitigated using tapered Hamming windows).
  • 2. Autoregressive (AR) Model (Parametric Estimator): Assumes the speech signal is generated by driving an all-pole linear filter with white noise excitation $e[n]$: $$x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] + e[n]$$ Why AR is superior for speech: Instead of estimating thousands of noisy individual frequency bins, the AR model estimates just $p$ coefficients ($a_1, \dots, a_p$). This fits a mathematically smooth spectral envelope that tracks human vocal tract formants without noise spikes. It is the theoretical bedrock of Linear Predictive Coding (LPC).
  • 3. Biased vs. Unbiased Autocorrelation Estimators: When computing sample autocorrelation from finite records:
    • Biased Estimator ($\hat{R}_b$): Divides by constant frame length $N$: $$\hat{R}_b(k) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] x[n+k]$$ Advantage: Strictly guarantees a positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix and non-negative energy spectrum, ensuring all-pole filter stability in Levinson-Durbin.
    • Unbiased Estimator ($\hat{R}_u$): Divides by actual number of overlapping terms $(N - k)$: $$\hat{R}_u(k) = \frac{1}{N - k} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n] x[n+k]$$ Drawback: For large lags $k$, $N-k$ becomes small, leading to massive statistical variance that can yield negative power estimates.
Worked Numerical Example: Computing Biased vs Unbiased Autocorrelation
Given 5-sample frame: $x = [3, 1, -2, 4, 2]$ ($N = 5$). Compute for lags $k = 0, 1, 2$:
Unscaled Cross-Product Sums $\sum x[n]x[n+k]$:
• Lag $k = 0$: $3^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2 + 4^2 + 2^2 = 9 + 1 + 4 + 16 + 4 = \mathbf{34}$
• Lag $k = 1$: $(3 \times 1) + (1 \times -2) + (-2 \times 4) + (4 \times 2) = 3 - 2 - 8 + 8 = \mathbf{1}$
• Lag $k = 2$: $(3 \times -2) + (1 \times 4) + (-2 \times 2) = -6 + 4 - 4 = \mathbf{-6}$
Biased Estimates (Divide always by $N = 5$):
• $\hat{R}_b(0) = 34 / 5 = \mathbf{6.8}$
• $\hat{R}_b(1) = 1 / 5 = \mathbf{0.2}$
• $\hat{R}_b(2) = -6 / 5 = \mathbf{-1.2}$
→ $\hat{R}_b = [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]$
Unbiased Estimates (Divide by $N - k$):
• $\hat{R}_u(0) = 34 / (5 - 0) = 34 / 5 = \mathbf{6.8}$
• $\hat{R}_u(1) = 1 / (5 - 1) = 1 / 4 = \mathbf{0.25}$
• $\hat{R}_u(2) = -6 / (5 - 2) = -6 / 3 = \mathbf{-2.0}$
→ $\hat{R}_u = [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]$
Result: Biased $\hat{R}_b = [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]$; Unbiased $\hat{R}_u = [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]$. Biased is always preferred for LPC stability!

Q8. Explain Linear Prediction, derive the Yule-Walker normal equations, and solve an order-2 prediction example.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Prediction principle, prediction error filter block diagram, full derivation of the Yule-Walker normal equations, and a complete order-2 matrix solution.

Definition: Linear Prediction (LP) is an analysis technique where the current speech sample $x[n]$ is estimated as a linear combination of the past $p$ samples: $$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k]$$ The difference between the actual speech sample and the predicted value is the Prediction Error (Residual): $$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n] = x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k]$$
  • Why Speech is Highly Predictable: Human speech organs (tongue, lips, jaw) possess physical mass and inertia; they cannot change position instantaneously. Consequently, adjacent speech samples exhibit strong mutual correlation. A properly configured linear predictor removes this redundancy, leaving a residual $e[n]$ with much lower variance (close to uncorrelated white noise).
  • Step-by-Step Derivation of Yule-Walker Normal Equations: The objective is to find the optimal prediction coefficients $\{a_k\}$ that minimize the Total Squared Prediction Error $E$: $$E = \sum_{n} e^2[n] = \sum_{n} \left( x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] \right)^2$$ To find the minimum, take the partial derivative of $E$ with respect to each predictor coefficient $a_i$ (for $i = 1, 2, \dots, p$) and set it equal to zero: $$\frac{\partial E}{\partial a_i} = -2 \sum_{n} \left( x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] \right) x[n-i] = 0$$ Dividing by $-2$ and distributing $x[n-i]$: $$\sum_{n} x[n] x[n-i] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k \sum_{n} x[n-k] x[n-i] = 0$$ Recognizing the sample autocorrelation definition $R(i) = \sum_n x[n] x[n-i]$ and $R(i-k) = \sum_n x[n-k] x[n-i]$, this simplifies directly into the Yule-Walker Normal Equations: $$\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k R(i-k) = R(i), \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, \dots, p$$
  • Matrix Representation ($p = 2$): $$\begin{bmatrix} R(0) & R(1) \\ R(1) & R(0) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R(1) \\ R(2) \end{bmatrix}$$ The minimum residual error energy at order $p$ is: $$E_p = R(0) - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k R(k)$$
Worked Numerical Example: Solving Order-2 Yule-Walker Normal Equations
Given autocorrelation values: $R(0) = 5.0$, $R(1) = 3.0$, $R(2) = 1.0$. Predictor order $p = 2$.
Construct matrix system: $$\begin{bmatrix} 5.0 & 3.0 \\ 3.0 & 5.0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 1.0 \end{bmatrix}$$
Calculate determinant $\Delta$: $$\Delta = (5.0 \times 5.0) - (3.0 \times 3.0) = 25.0 - 9.0 = \mathbf{16.0}$$
Apply Cramer's Rule to solve for $a_1$ and $a_2$: $$a_1 = \frac{(3.0 \times 5.0) - (1.0 \times 3.0)}{\Delta} = \frac{15.0 - 3.0}{16.0} = \frac{12.0}{16.0} = \mathbf{0.75}$$ $$a_2 = \frac{(5.0 \times 1.0) - (3.0 \times 3.0)}{\Delta} = \frac{5.0 - 9.0}{16.0} = \frac{-4.0}{16.0} = \mathbf{-0.25}$$
Compute Minimum Residual Energy $E_2$: $$E_2 = R(0) - a_1 R(1) - a_2 R(2) = 5.0 - (0.75 \times 3.0) - (-0.25 \times 1.0)$$ $$E_2 = 5.0 - 2.25 + 0.25 = \mathbf{3.0}$$
Final Solution: Predictor Coefficients $a_1 = 0.75$, $a_2 = -0.25$; Minimum Residual Energy $E_2 = 3.0$.
Mnemonic "Past predicts Future; Minimize Error to get Yule-Walker".
If it comes as a 4-mark question: Write the definition, draw Fig 2.1, state the Yule-Walker formula, and write the final numeric answers ($a_1=0.75$, $a_2=-0.25$, $E_2=3.0$).
Quick recall LP predicts sample from $p$ past samples: $\hat{x}[n] = \sum a_k x[n-k]$. Minimizing squared error $\partial E/\partial a_i = 0$ yields $\sum a_k R(i-k) = R(i)$. Example gives $a_1=0.75, a_2=-0.25, E_2=3.0$.

Q9. Explain Prediction Gain and Segmental Prediction Gain, and compute the prediction gain for a given speech frame.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Formal definition and physical interpretation of Prediction Gain, short-time framing pipeline, Segmental Prediction Gain (SPG), and a full numerical computation.

Definition: Prediction Gain ($G_p$) is a fundamental quantitative metric measuring the compression efficiency of a linear predictor. It is defined as the logarithmic ratio of the input speech signal variance (power) to the prediction error (residual) variance: $$G_p = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}\right)\ \text{dB}$$ where $\sigma_x^2$ is the variance (average power) of original speech $x[n]$, and $\sigma_e^2$ is the variance of residual error $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$.
  • Physical Meaning of Prediction Gain: Prediction Gain reflects how much redundant energy has been stripped away from the speech signal:
    • High $G_p$ (10 to 15 dB): Indicates strong correlation (typical of voiced vowels). The predictor successfully guesses almost the entire signal, leaving a tiny residual error that can be transmitted using very few bits.
    • Low $G_p$ (0 to 3 dB): Indicates little or no correlation (typical of unvoiced fricatives like /s/ or background noise). The residual error power is nearly as large as the original signal.
  • Segmental Prediction Gain (SPG): Speech is non-stationary; high-energy voiced frames would dominate a simple global average. Segmental Prediction Gain computes $G_p$ in decibels for each frame $m$ independently, then averages the dB values across all $M$ frames: $$\text{SPG} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} G_p(m) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_{x,m}^2}{\sigma_{e,m}^2}\right)\ \text{dB}$$
Worked Numerical Example: Computing Prediction Gain for Frame A (Voiced Speech)
Given 8-sample speech frame: $x_A = [2, 4, 6, 5, 3, 1, -1, -2]$, predictor order $p = 1$.
Compute Autocorrelation values $R(0)$ and $R(1)$:
• $R(0) = \sum_{n=0}^7 x^2[n] = 2^2 + 4^2 + 6^2 + 5^2 + 3^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + (-2)^2 = 4 + 16 + 36 + 25 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 4 = \mathbf{96}$
• $R(1) = \sum_{n=1}^7 x[n]x[n-1] = (4 \times 2) + (6 \times 4) + (5 \times 6) + (3 \times 5) + (1 \times 3) + (-1 \times 1) + (-2 \times -1)$
• $R(1) = 8 + 24 + 30 + 15 + 3 - 1 + 2 = \mathbf{81}$
Optimal order-1 coefficient: $a_1 = \dfrac{R(1)}{R(0)} = \dfrac{81}{96} \approx \mathbf{0.844}$
Original signal power: $\sigma_x^2 = \dfrac{R(0)}{N} = \dfrac{96}{8} = \mathbf{12.0}$
Compute residual samples $e[n] = x[n] - 0.844 x[n-1]$ for $n = 1$ to $7$:
• $e[1] = 4 - 0.844(2) = 2.312$ → $e^2[1] \approx 5.35$
• $e[2] = 6 - 0.844(4) = 2.624$ → $e^2[2] \approx 6.89$
• $e[3] = 5 - 0.844(6) = -0.064$ → $e^2[3] \approx 0.00$
• $e[4] = 3 - 0.844(5) = -1.220$ → $e^2[4] \approx 1.49$
• $e[5] = 1 - 0.844(3) = -1.532$ → $e^2[5] \approx 2.35$
• $e[6] = -1 - 0.844(1) = -1.844$ → $e^2[6] \approx 3.40$
• $e[7] = -2 - 0.844(-1) = -1.156$ → $e^2[7] \approx 1.34$
Sum of squared errors $\sum_{n=1}^7 e^2[n] \approx 20.82$
Residual error power: $\sigma_e^2 = \dfrac{20.82}{7} \approx \mathbf{2.97}$
Calculate Prediction Gain in decibels: $$G_p = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}\right) = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{12.0}{2.97}\right) = 10\log_{10}(4.04) \approx 10 \times 0.606 = \mathbf{6.06\text{ dB}}$$

Quick recall $G_p = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2)$ dB. Measures power reduction achieved by predictor. Voiced speech yields high gain (10–15 dB); unvoiced yields low gain (0–3 dB). SPG averages dB gains across frames.

Q10. Explain the Levinson-Durbin algorithm and reflection coefficients, and solve a step-by-step numerical example.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Why the algorithm exists ($O(p^2)$ vs $O(p^3)$), recursion flowchart, reflection coefficients ($k_m$) stability condition, and a full order-2 step-by-step numerical calculation.

Definition: The Levinson-Durbin algorithm is an elegant, computationally efficient recursive algorithm that solves the Yule-Walker normal equations in $O(p^2)$ operations (compared to $O(p^3)$ for standard Gaussian elimination). It exploits the symmetric Toeplitz structure of the autocorrelation matrix to compute prediction coefficients $a_k$ alongside intermediate reflection coefficients $k_m$ and residual energies $E_m$ without matrix inversion.
  • Why Levinson-Durbin is Essential: Inverting an arbitrary $p \times p$ matrix requires $O(p^3)$ multiplications. In a real-time cell phone transmitting speech, calculating an order-10 or order-16 LPC filter 50 times per second would drain phone batteries and introduce lag. By exploiting the fact that the autocorrelation matrix is Toeplitz (every diagonal is constant: $R_{i,j} = R(|i-j|)$), Levinson-Durbin solves the system recursively in only $O(p^2)$ operations!
  • Reflection Coefficients ($k_m$) and Filter Stability: Also called PARCOR (Partial Correlation) coefficients.
    Critical Stability Rule: The synthesized all-pole filter is guaranteed to be strictly stable if and only if: $$|k_m| < 1, \quad \text{for all } m = 1, 2, \dots, p$$ If even a single $|k_m| \ge 1$, the residual error energy $E_m = (1 - k_m^2)E_{m-1}$ becomes zero or negative, indicating unstable poles outside the unit circle.
  • Order-Update Formula (New from Old): To step from order $m-1$ to order $m$: $$a_m^{(m)} = k_m$$ $$a_j^{(m)} = a_j^{(m-1)} - k_m \cdot a_{m-j}^{(m-1)}, \quad \text{for } j = 1, 2, \dots, m-1$$
Worked Numerical Example: Full Order-2 Levinson-Durbin Recursion
Given: $R(0) = 5.0$, $R(1) = 3.0$, $R(2) = 1.0$. Desired model order $p = 2$.
Step 0: Initialization ($m = 0$):
Initial error energy: $E_0 = R(0) = \mathbf{5.0}$.
Step 1: First Iteration ($m = 1$):
• Compute reflection coefficient $k_1$: $$k_1 = \frac{R(1)}{E_0} = \frac{3.0}{5.0} = \mathbf{0.6} \quad (|0.6| < 1 \implies \text{STABLE})$$ • Update order-1 predictor coefficient: $$a_1^{(1)} = k_1 = \mathbf{0.6}$$ • Update residual error energy $E_1$: $$E_1 = (1 - k_1^2) E_0 = (1 - 0.6^2) \times 5.0 = (1 - 0.36) \times 5.0 = 0.64 \times 5.0 = \mathbf{3.2}$$
Step 2: Second Iteration ($m = 2$):
• Compute reflection coefficient $k_2$: $$k_2 = \frac{R(2) - a_1^{(1)} R(1)}{E_1} = \frac{1.0 - (0.6 \times 3.0)}{3.2} = \frac{1.0 - 1.8}{3.2} = \frac{-0.8}{3.2} = \mathbf{-0.25} \quad (|-0.25| < 1 \implies \text{STABLE})$$ • Update order-2 predictor coefficients: $$a_2^{(2)} = k_2 = \mathbf{-0.25}$$ $$a_1^{(2)} = a_1^{(1)} - k_2 a_1^{(1)} = 0.6 - (-0.25 \times 0.6) = 0.6 + 0.15 = \mathbf{0.75}$$ • Update residual error energy $E_2$: $$E_2 = (1 - k_2^2) E_1 = (1 - (-0.25)^2) \times 3.2 = (1 - 0.0625) \times 3.2 = 0.9375 \times 3.2 = \mathbf{3.0}$$
Final Output: Predictor coefficients $a_1 = 0.75$, $a_2 = -0.25$; Reflection coefficients $k_1 = 0.6$, $k_2 = -0.25$; Final Residual Energy $E_2 = 3.0$ (Matches direct matrix inversion from Q8!).
If it comes as a 4-mark question: Write the definition, draw Fig 2.3, give the $|k_m| < 1$ stability condition, and complete Iteration 1 ($k_1=0.6, a_1^{(1)}=0.6, E_1=3.2$).
Quick recall Levinson-Durbin solves Yule-Walker in $O(p^2)$ without matrix inversion. Inputs: $R(0..p)$; Outputs: $a_k$, $k_m$, $E_p$. Stability guaranteed iff all $|k_m| < 1$. $E_m$ strictly decreases as order increases.

Q11. Compare Short-Term and Long-Term linear prediction models, and explain Moving-Average prediction, contrasting AR and MA.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Cascaded STP + LTP prediction architecture, mathematical representations, Moving-Average (MA) formulation, and a comprehensive AR vs MA comparison table.

Definition: In speech coding, linear prediction is partitioned into two specialized cascaded stages: Short-Term Prediction (STP) (order $p \approx 10\text{--}16$) which removes vocal tract formant resonances, and Long-Term Prediction (LTP) which removes vocal fold pitch periodicity. Conversely, a Moving-Average (MA) model predicts the current sample as a linear combination of past excitation residuals/errors rather than past speech samples: $x[n] = e[n] + \sum_{k=1}^q b_k e[n-k]$.
  • Short-Term Linear Prediction (STP — Formant Filter): Predicts $x[n]$ using the immediately preceding samples ($x[n-1], \dots, x[n-p]$) spanning ~1 to 2 milliseconds.
    Filter Formula: $A(z) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k z^{-k}$ (typically order $p \approx 10$ for 8 kHz speech).
    Job: Flattens the spectral envelope by eliminating formant peaks.
  • Long-Term Linear Prediction (LTP — Pitch Filter): Takes the short-term residual $e[n]$ and removes pitch periodicity spanning 5 to 15 milliseconds.
    Filter Formula: $P(z) = 1 - \beta z^{-T_0}$, where $T_0$ is the pitch period lag and $\beta$ is the pitch predictor gain.
    Job: Eliminates harmonic pitch spikes, producing a completely white, uncorrelated final residual $r[n]$ (Fig 2.4).
  • Moving Average (MA) Prediction Model: While an AR model expresses $x[n]$ as a function of past outputs, an MA model expresses $x[n]$ as a weighted sum of current and past input noise errors: $$x[n] = e[n] + b_1 e[n-1] + b_2 e[n-2] + \dots + b_q e[n-q]$$ Transfer Function: $H(z) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^q b_k z^{-k}$.
    Properties: It is an all-zero FIR filter. Because it lacks a recursive feedback loop, an MA model is always unconditionally BIBO stable!
  • Why AR Models Dominate Speech Processing: Human vocal tract formants are sharp, high-energy resonance peaks. An AR (all-pole) model produces sharp resonant peaks using very few parameters ($p \approx 10$) and can be solved using simple linear algebra (Yule-Walker). An MA model can only model spectral nulls/dips; modeling sharp peaks with an MA filter requires an impractically high filter order ($q > 50$) and non-linear optimization!

Q12. Explain Scalar Quantization: Uniform, Optimum Lloyd-Max, Logarithmic Companding, and Adaptive/Differential quantizers.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Quantizer staircase characteristics, SQNR 6 dB rule, Lloyd-Max optimality conditions, logarithmic companding ($\mu$-law / A-law), and DPCM / ADPCM.

Definition: Scalar Quantization is the process of mapping a continuous-amplitude analog sample $x$ to a discrete amplitude level $y_k = Q(x)$ chosen from a finite codebook of reconstruction levels, bounded by decision thresholds $d_k$. Companding non-linearly compresses dynamic range to protect quiet sounds. DPCM quantizes the difference between actual and predicted samples to achieve compression.
  • 1. Uniform Quantization & The 6 dB Rule: Decision boundaries and reconstruction levels are equally spaced by a constant step size $\Delta = \frac{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}{L}$, where $L = 2^B$ levels for $B$ bits (Fig 2.5).
    Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio (SQNR): Assuming a full-scale sinusoidal input: $$\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02 B + 1.76\ \text{dB}$$ Golden Rule of Quantization: Every single bit added to a quantizer increases SQNR by approximately 6 dB!
  • 2. Optimum Non-Uniform Quantizer (Lloyd-Max Conditions): Uniform quantizers waste resolution when input signals are not uniformly distributed. Human speech has a Laplacian (peaked) probability density function (PDF)—quiet sounds occur far more frequently than loud sounds.
    The Lloyd-Max quantizer minimizes Mean Squared Error (MSE) by satisfying two simultaneous conditions:
    1. Nearest-Neighbor Condition: Decision boundary $d_k$ is the exact midpoint between adjacent reconstruction levels: $$d_k = \frac{y_k + y_{k+1}}{2}$$
    2. Centroid Condition: Reconstruction level $y_k$ is the conditional mean (center of mass) of the input PDF over that interval: $$y_k = \frac{\int_{d_{k-1}}^{d_k} x p(x)\,dx}{\int_{d_{k-1}}^{d_k} p(x)\,dx}$$
  • 3. Logarithmic Companding ($\mu$-law & A-law): Hardware implementation of Lloyd-Max is complex. Telephony instead uses Companding (Compressing + Expanding): a non-linear logarithmic curve compresses the signal dynamic range before uniform quantization, and an inverse curve expands it at the receiver.
    • $\mu$-law (North America & Japan — $\mu = 255$): $$C(x) = \text{sgn}(x) \frac{\ln(1 + \mu |x|)}{\ln(1 + \mu)}, \quad |x| \le 1$$
    • A-law (Europe & India — $A = 87.6$): Used in international PSTN networks, providing nearly constant SQNR across a wide 40 dB dynamic range.
  • 4. Differential Quantization (DPCM & ADPCM):
    • DPCM (Differential PCM): Because adjacent speech samples are heavily correlated, their difference $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$ has much smaller amplitude range than $x[n]$. Quantizing the difference allows 8-bit quality to be achieved with only 4 bits!
    • ADPCM (Adaptive DPCM — ITU-T G.726 @ 32 kbps): Adapts both the quantizer step size $\Delta[n]$ and predictor coefficients dynamically to local signal volume.
Worked Numerical Example: Uniform SQNR and $\mu$-Law Compression
Part A — 3-Bit Uniform Quantizer:
Number of levels: $L = 2^3 = 8$ levels.
Calculate theoretical SQNR: $$\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02(3) + 1.76 = 18.06 + 1.76 = \mathbf{19.82\text{ dB}}$$
Part B — $\mu$-Law Compression for Normalized Input $x = 0.5$ ($\mu = 255$): $$C(0.5) = \frac{\ln(1 + 255 \times 0.5)}{\ln(1 + 255)} = \frac{\ln(1 + 127.5)}{\ln(256)} = \frac{\ln(128.5)}{\ln(256)}$$
Using natural logarithms: $\ln(128.5) \approx 4.8559$; $\ln(256) \approx 5.5452$. $$C(0.5) = \frac{4.8559}{5.5452} \approx \mathbf{0.8757}$$
Results: 3-Bit Uniform SQNR = 19.82 dB; $\mu$-Law compresses normalized amplitude 0.5 up to 0.8757 (amplifying weak signals).

Quick recall Uniform step $\Delta$; SQNR gains ~6 dB per added bit. Lloyd-Max: boundaries at midpoints, levels at centroids. $\mu$-law / A-law companding boosts weak signals to maintain constant SQNR. DPCM quantizes prediction error $e[n]$ to save bits.

Q13. Explain Vector Quantization, distortion measures, and the LBG algorithm for codebook design.

Unit II · 8 marks · Exam focus: Scalar vs Vector Quantization, distortion measures (Euclidean, Itakura-Saito), LBG algorithm flowchart, and a complete 2D centroid numerical update.

Definition: Vector Quantization (VQ) is an advanced lossy compression technique where an ordered group of $k$ consecutive samples is bundled into a single $k$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k]^T$. The vector is compared against a pre-computed codebook $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2, \dots, \mathbf{y}_N\}$, mapped to the closest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$, and only the codevector's integer index $i$ is transmitted over the channel.
  • Why Vector Quantization Outperforms Scalar Quantization: According to Shannon's Rate-Distortion Theory, quantizing vectors as a multidimensional group always achieves lower distortion for a given bit rate than quantizing each scalar sample individually, because VQ exploits:
    1. Linear correlation between consecutive samples.
    2. Non-linear statistical dependencies across samples.
    3. Multidimensional geometric packing advantages (spherical packing is denser than rectilinear grids).
  • Common Distortion Measures $d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$: Quantifies the error or distance between input vector $\mathbf{x}$ and codevector $\mathbf{y}$:
    • Squared Euclidean Distance (MSE): $$d_{\text{MSE}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} (x_j - y_j)^2 = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|^2$$
    • Weighted Euclidean Distance: $d_W(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{W} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})$, weighting perceptually sensitive frequency bands higher.
    • Itakura-Saito Distortion: Specialized spectral distortion measure used to compare LPC all-pole speech envelopes.
  • The LBG Algorithm (Generalized Lloyd Algorithm) (Fig 2.10): Iterative clustering algorithm used to design an optimal codebook from a large training database of speech vectors:
    1. Step 1 — Initialize: Choose an initial codebook $\mathcal{C}_0 = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_N\}$ (e.g. by vector splitting or random selection).
    2. Step 2 — Partition (Voronoi Assignment): Assign each training vector $\mathbf{x}_m$ to its nearest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$ using the minimum distortion rule: $$R_i = \{ \mathbf{x} : d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_i) \le d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_j), \forall j \ne i \}$$
    3. Step 3 — Update Centroids: Recompute each codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$ as the geometric center of mass (centroid / mean) of all training vectors assigned to cell $R_i$: $$\mathbf{y}_i^* = \frac{1}{|R_i|} \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in R_i} \mathbf{x}$$
    4. Step 4 — Check Convergence: Calculate total average distortion $D_{\text{new}}$. If $\frac{D_{\text{old}} - D_{\text{new}}}{D_{\text{old}}} < \epsilon$ (where $\epsilon \approx 0.001$), stop and output codebook; otherwise set $D_{\text{old}} = D_{\text{new}}$ and loop back to Step 2.
Worked Numerical Example: 2D LBG Centroid Update Calculation
Given 4 training speech vectors in 2D space:
• $P_1 = (1, 2)$   • $P_2 = (2, 4)$   • $P_3 = (7, 8)$   • $P_4 = (9, 10)$
Initial codevectors: $C_1 = (4, 5)$ and $C_2 = (5, 7)$.
Partitioning Stage: Calculate squared Euclidean distance to each codevector:
• For $P_1(1,2)$:
  $d(P_1, C_1) = (1-4)^2 + (2-5)^2 = 9 + 9 = 18$
  $d(P_1, C_2) = (1-5)^2 + (2-7)^2 = 16 + 25 = 41 \implies \mathbf{P_1 \to R_1}$ (closer to $C_1$)
• For $P_2(2,4)$:
  $d(P_2, C_1) = (2-4)^2 + (4-5)^2 = 4 + 1 = 5$
  $d(P_2, C_2) = (2-5)^2 + (4-7)^2 = 9 + 9 = 18 \implies \mathbf{P_2 \to R_1}$ (closer to $C_1$)
• For $P_3(7,8)$:
  $d(P_3, C_1) = (7-4)^2 + (8-5)^2 = 9 + 9 = 18$

1-Mark Essential Questions (Rapid Flashcards)

25 high-frequency questions for the compulsory 6 × 1-mark section. Master the core formula, definition, and rapid diagram for each question to score 6/6.

Q1

Differentiate voiced and unvoiced speech sounds.

Voiced: Vocal folds vibrate periodically, producing quasi-periodic glottal pulses (e.g. vowels /a/, /i/).
Unvoiced: Vocal folds remain open; turbulent airflow creates random noise excitation (e.g. fricatives /s/, /f/).
Exam Key: Voiced = periodic glottal pulses; Unvoiced = turbulent white noise.
Q2

What is the Source-Filter Model of speech production?

Represents speech as an acoustic excitation source (periodic impulse train or white noise) scaled by gain $G$, filtered by a linear time-varying vocal tract filter $H(z) = 1/A(z)$ to produce speech $s[n]$.
Exam Key: Speech = Excitation Source × Vocal Tract Filter.
Q3

State human hearing and telephone speech frequency bands.

Human hearing: 20 Hz to 20 kHz (20,000 Hz).
Telephone speech band: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (standard 8 kHz sampling rate, 4 kHz Nyquist bandwidth).
Exam Key: Audible = 20 Hz–20 kHz · Telephone = 300–3400 Hz (8 kHz fs).
Q4

Classify speech coding techniques into three major categories.

(1) Waveform Coders: PCM, ADPCM (16–64 kbps, preserve waveform shape).
(2) Hybrid Coders: CELP, ACELP (4.8–16 kbps, cellular standard).
(3) Parametric/Vocoders: LPC-10 (1.2–2.4 kbps, synthetic quality).
Exam Key: Waveform (PCM) → Hybrid (CELP) → Vocoder (LPC).
Q5

What is Mean Opinion Score (MOS)?

ITU-T subjective speech quality metric rated by human listeners on a 1 to 5 scale: 5 (Excellent), 4 (Good), 3 (Fair), 2 (Poor), 1 (Bad). Commercial PSTN toll quality requires MOS ≥ 4.0.
Exam Key: 1–5 scale · PSTN Toll Quality requires MOS ≥ 4.0.
Q6

Define pitch period and fundamental frequency ($F_0$).

Pitch period ($T_0$): Time elapsed in seconds for one complete vocal-fold vibration cycle.
Fundamental frequency ($F_0$): Vibration rate $F_0 = 1 / T_0$ (Men: ~120 Hz, Women: ~220 Hz, Kids: ~300 Hz).
Exam Key: $F_0 = 1/T_0$ · Male ~120 Hz · Female ~220 Hz.
Q7

Differentiate All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) filters.

All-Pole (IIR): $H(z) = G / A(z)$, contains feedback; models vocal tract resonance peaks (formants).
All-Zero (FIR): $H(z) = B(z)$, feedforward only; poles at $z=0$, models spectral nulls and nasals.
Exam Key: All-pole = IIR (formant peaks); All-zero = FIR (spectral dips).
Q8

State the discrete-time convolution formula.

$y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k]\,h[n-k]$. Four operational steps: Flip $h[k]$, Shift by $n$, Multiply overlapping samples, and Sum. Output length $= M+N-1$.
Exam Key: 4 steps: Flip, Shift, Multiply, Sum · Length = $M+N-1$.
Q9

What is the Wiener-Khinchin theorem?

States that for any wide-sense stationary (WSS) discrete-time random process, the Power Spectral Density (PSD) $S_{xx}(e^{j\omega})$ is the discrete-time Fourier transform of its autocorrelation sequence $r_{xx}[k]$.
Exam Key: Autocorrelation $\xleftrightarrow{\text{FT}}$ Power Spectral Density (PSD).
Q10

Differentiate biased and unbiased autocorrelation estimators.

Biased: Divides by frame length $N$ ($\hat{r}_b[k] = \frac{1}{N}\sum x[n]x[n+k]$); guarantees a positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix.
Unbiased: Divides by $N-|k|$; higher variance at large lags, can yield negative energy.
Exam Key: Biased $\div N$ guarantees positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix.
Q11

List the four types of speech production models.

(1) Physiological model: Simulates vocal cords, muscles, and airflow mechanics.
(2) Acoustic model: Source-filter lossless acoustic tube concats.
(3) Psycholinguistic model: Concept-to-articulatory control chain.
(4) LPC model: Mathematical all-pole linear predictive model.
Exam Key: 4 models: Physiological, Acoustic, Psycholinguistic, LPC.
Q12

Define poles and zeros. Why is an FIR filter always stable?

Zeros: Roots of numerator of $H(z)$ where gain drops to 0.
Poles: Roots of denominator where gain goes to $\infty$.
FIR filters have all $M-1$ poles at the origin ($z=0$), which is strictly inside the unit circle ($|z| < 1$), ensuring BIBO stability.
Exam Key: FIR poles are all at origin ($z=0$) inside unit circle &implies; always stable.
Q13

Define Linear Prediction in speech processing.

An estimation method predicting the current speech sample $x[n]$ as a linear combination of $p$ past samples: $\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^p a_k x[n-k]$. The prediction error is $e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$.
Exam Key: $\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^p a_k x[n-k]$ with LP order $p \approx 10\text{--}14$.
Q14

Define Prediction Gain (PG).

Ratio of input signal variance (power) to prediction error variance (power):
$G_p = 10\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}\right)\text{ dB}$. Measures redundancy reduction. Voiced speech yields 10–15 dB.
Exam Key: $G_p = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2)$ dB · Voiced: 10–15 dB.
Q15

State computational complexity of Levinson-Durbin algorithm.

Solves $p$-th order Yule-Walker Toeplitz equations recursively in $O(p^2)$ operations, compared to $O(p^3)$ for Gaussian elimination, making real-time LPC synthesis possible on DSP chips.
Exam Key: Complexity $O(p^2)$ vs direct matrix inversion $O(p^3)$.
Q16

What condition on reflection coefficients ($k_m$) ensures filter stability?

The all-pole synthesis filter $1/A(z)$ is minimum-phase (strictly stable) if and only if $|k_m| < 1$ for all stages $m = 1, 2, \dots, p$. If $|k_m| \ge 1$, poles lie on or outside the unit circle.
Exam Key: Stability condition: $|k_m| < 1$ for all $m=1,2,\dots,p$.
Q17

Differentiate short-term and long-term linear prediction.

Short-Term LP ($p \approx 10$): Removes sample-to-sample correlations (~1 ms); models vocal tract formant resonances.
Long-Term LP (Pitch Filter): Removes cycle-to-cycle periodicity (~5–15 ms); models pitch period $T_0$.
Exam Key: STP = Formant envelope ($p \approx 10$); LTP = Pitch periodicity ($T_0$).
Q18

What is Moving Average (MA) prediction?

Predicting the current sample from a linear combination of past prediction error/residual signals rather than past speech samples: $x[n] = e[n] + \sum_{k=1}^q b_k e[n-k]$. Corresponds to an all-zero FIR filter.
Exam Key: MA predicts from past errors (all-zero FIR filter).
Q19

State the SQNR formula for a $B$-bit uniform quantizer.

$\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02 B + 1.76\text{ dB}$ for a full-scale sinusoidal input. Key takeaway: Each additional bit adds approximately 6 dB of Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio.
Exam Key: $\text{SQNR} \approx 6.02 B + 1.76\text{ dB}$ → +6 dB per added bit.
Q20

State the two Lloyd-Max optimum quantization conditions.

(1) Nearest-Neighbor: Decision boundary $d_k = \frac{y_k + y_{k+1}}{2}$ is the midpoint of adjacent reconstruction levels.
(2) Centroid Condition: Reconstruction level $y_k$ is the conditional mean (center of mass) of PDF over interval $[d_{k-1}, d_k]$.
Exam Key: $d_k = (y_k + y_{k+1})/2$ and $y_k = \text{centroid of interval } [d_{k-1}, d_k]$.
Q21

What is Vector Quantization (VQ)?

Quantizing a block of $k$ continuous samples together as a single vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \dots, x_k]^T$ by mapping it to the closest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$ in codebook $\mathcal{C}$ and transmitting only index $i$. Exploits inter-sample correlation.
Exam Key: $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^k \to$ nearest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i \to$ send index $i$.
Q22

What is the LBG algorithm?

Linde-Buzo-Gray algorithm: iterative codebook design alternating two steps: (1) Voronoi Partition: assign training vectors to nearest codevector; (2) Centroid Update: recalculate codevector as mean of assigned vectors. Repeats until distortion reduction < $\epsilon$.
Exam Key: LBG = Voronoi Partition → Centroid Update → Repeat until convergence.
Q23

What is a Toeplitz matrix? Why does Levinson-Durbin use it?

A symmetric matrix whose diagonals parallel to main diagonal are constant ($R_{i,j} = r_{|i-j|}$). The LPC autocorrelation matrix is Toeplitz, allowing the Levinson-Durbin recursion to solve the linear system in $O(p^2)$ time instead of $O(p^3)$.
Exam Key: Constant diagonals ($R_{i,j} = r_{|i-j|}$) &implies; solves in $O(p^2)$.
Q24

Differentiate AR and MA models.

AR (Autoregressive): All-pole IIR filter; output depends on past outputs; models sharp formant resonance peaks.
MA (Moving Average): All-zero FIR filter; output depends on past inputs/errors; models spectral dips, nulls, and nasals.
Exam Key: AR = All-pole / IIR (formants); MA = All-zero / FIR (spectral dips).
Q25

Differentiate forward (AQF) and backward (AQB) adaptive quantization.

AQF (Forward): Step size $\Delta$ is calculated from unquantized input frame and transmitted as side information (adds delay and bit overhead).
AQB (Backward): Step size $\Delta$ is estimated from past quantized output (zero side info; sensitive to channel bit errors).
Exam Key: AQF requires side info transmission; AQB estimates from output (zero side info).
