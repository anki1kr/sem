Q1. Explain the human speech production mechanism and the Source-Filter model with a neat diagram.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the three production stages, the components table, voiced/unvoiced contrast, and the source-filter block diagram.

Definition: Speech production is the physiological process where airflow from the lungs passes through the vocal cords (larynx) to create an acoustic excitation source, which is then filtered by the vocal tract (pharynx, oral and nasal cavities) to produce spoken sounds. The Source-Filter Model represents speech $s[n]$ as the output of a linear time-varying vocal tract filter $H(z)$ excited by either a periodic impulse train (voiced) or white noise (unvoiced).
  • Three-stage process: 1. Conceptualization (choosing what to say) → 2. Formulation (words and grammar) → 3. Articulation (lungs, vocal cords, tongue physically produce the sound).
  • Key components: Lungs (air reservoir, energy source); Vocal cords in the larynx (vibrate for voiced sounds, stay open for unvoiced); Vocal tract (pharynx, oral, nasal cavity – acts as the resonating filter); Articulators (tongue, lips, jaw, velum – change the vocal tract shape).
  • Voiced sounds: vocal cords vibrate quasi-periodically (vowels /a/, /e/); has a pitch period $T_0$. Unvoiced sounds: vocal cords stay open, turbulent air makes noise (fricatives /s/, /f/); no vibration.
  • Source-Filter equation: $s[n]$ = excitation (impulse train or white noise) $\times$ Gain $G$, filtered by $H(z)$, the vocal tract transfer function.
  • Link to LPC: LPC is the mathematical form of the source-filter idea – its all-pole filter stands in for the vocal tract, $\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$.
  • Types of production models: Physiological (models the body), Acoustic/Source-Filter (models the sound), Psycholinguistic (models the brain stages above), LPC (models the maths).

Mnemonic Stages CFA: Conceptualize → Formulate → Articulate. Models PAPL (Papa Aaj Padhenge LPC): Physiological, Acoustic, Psycholinguistic, LPC.

Q2. Describe the human auditory system: parts of the ear, the pathway to the brain, and how hearing works.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the three-part ear diagram, the parts/jobs table, hearing range figures, and the pathway to the brain.

Definition: The Human Auditory System processes acoustic sound pressure waves through three main anatomical regions – the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear (cochlea) – mapping frequencies along the basilar membrane for neural perception.
  • Human hearing range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; most sensitive between 1 kHz and 4 kHz. Telephone band: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (8 kHz sampling).
  • Cochlea & basilar membrane: acts as a biological frequency analyzer – high frequencies resonate near the stiff base, low frequencies near the flexible apex.
  • Auditory masking: a louder sound renders a softer adjacent-frequency sound inaudible; exploited by perceptual codecs.
  • How hearing works (5 steps): (1) sound waves vibrate the eardrum; (2) vibrations pass through the ossicles malleus → incus → stapes; (3) ossicles drive the fluid-filled cochlea; (4) hair cells convert motion to electrical signals sent along the auditory nerve; (5) the brain translates the signals into sound.
  • Pathway to the brain (in order):
    1. Hair cells (cochlea)
    2. Auditory nerve
    3. Cochlear nucleus
    4. Superior olivary nucleus – compares both ears
    5. Lateral lemniscus
    6. Inferior colliculus
    7. (Medial geniculate body)
    8. Auditory cortex in the temporal lobe

Q3. Explain the general structure of a speech coder and classify speech coding techniques (Waveform, Parametric, Hybrid) with examples.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the encoder/decoder block diagram, the classification tree, and the comparison table with the compression-ratio example.

Definition: A speech coder consists of an encoder that analyzes and quantizes input speech into a bitstream, and a decoder that synthesizes speech back from it. Speech coding techniques are classified into: (1) Waveform Coders, (2) Parametric Coders (Vocoders), and (3) Hybrid Coders.
  • Encoder stages: Input (digital speech) → Analysis (spectral envelope, pitch, voicing) → Coding (compact bitstream).
  • Decoder stages: Input (bitstream) → Decoding (drives a synthesis model) → Output (reconstructed speech).
  • Waveform coders: reproduce the waveform sample by sample – PCM (64 kbps), ADPCM (32 kbps), sub-band coding (SBC). High bit-rate, high quality, robust to noise.
  • Parametric coders (vocoders): send only model parameters – LPC-10 (2.4 kbps), formant coding. Very low bit-rate, synthetic/robotic quality.
  • Hybrid coders: LPC filter excited by a codebook – CELP, AMR, MBE. Medium bit-rate, near-toll quality.
  • Factors influencing classification: bit rate, complexity, quality, robustness (mnemonic BCQR).

Q4. Discuss the requirements of speech codecs: quality, coding delay, and robustness, with trade-offs.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the four-dimension diagram, the MOS/G.114 tables, and one worked numeric (SNR or delay).

Definition: The performance of a speech codec is governed by four core requirements: low bit rate, high perceptual quality, minimal processing delay, and high noise/channel-error robustness.
  • Quality: intelligibility, naturalness, absence of artifacts. Metrics: MOS (subjective, 1–5, toll quality ≥ 4.0), PESQ (objective), SD (spectral distortion), SNR (signal power vs error power).
  • Coding delay: algorithmic/frame delay (buffering, typically 10–30 ms) + processing delay + transmission delay. High delay ($>$150 ms) causes echo and disrupts turn-taking.
  • Robustness: noise robustness, reverberation robustness, packet-loss robustness. VAD (Voice Activity Detection) saves bits during silence; CNG (Comfort Noise Generation) plays soft noise so the line does not sound dead.
  • Trade-offs: higher quality costs more bit rate; lower bit rate costs some quality; more robustness (longer analysis windows) costs more delay.

150 to 400 msAcceptable depending on the impact
Above 400 msUnacceptable for general network planning

Original $x = [4, -2, 3, 1]$, decoded $\tilde{x} = [3.9, -2.1, 3.2, 0.9]$
Signal power sum: $16 + 4 + 9 + 1 = 30$. Error $= [0.1, 0.1, -0.2, 0.1]$; error power sum $= 0.07$
$SNR = 10\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{30}{0.07}\right) = 10\log_{10}(428.57) = 26.32$ dB

Q5. Explain the methods of pitch-period estimation and compute the pitch period for a given lag using autocorrelation.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the autocorrelation formula and curve, the methods comparison, and a computed $T_0$/$F_0$ example.

Definition: Pitch-period estimation is the process of determining the fundamental period $T_0$ (or fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$) of a voiced speech segment.
  • Fundamental frequency ranges: adult males 80–160 Hz, adult females 150–300 Hz, children 200–500 Hz.
  • Autocorrelation method: $R(k) = \sum_n x[n]\,x[n+k]$; for voiced speech, $R(k)$ shows a secondary peak at lag $k = P_0$, giving $T_0 = P_0/f_s$. $R(0)$ is always the largest, so the peak is searched only for $k \gt 0$.
  • AMDF (low-complexity alternative): $\text{AMDF}[k] = \frac{1}{N}\sum |x[n]-x[n+k]|$; pitch lag is found from a dip instead of a peak.
  • Cepstrum: $c[n] = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\log|X(\omega)|\}$; separates the vocal tract from the excitation, giving a clean peak at quefrency $T_0$.
  • SIFT: LP inverse filter removes the vocal tract first; autocorrelation of the residual gives an unambiguous pitch peak.
  • General procedure (4 stages):
    1. Pre-processing – filter, digitise.
    2. Framing + windowing – $x_m[n] = x[n]\,w[n-mL]$.
    3. Pitch detection.
    4. Post-processing – smooth the contour.
MethodHow $T_0$ is foundWeakness
AutocorrelationLargest peak of $R(k)$ after lag 0Formant peaks can be mistaken for pitch
CepstrumPeak at quefrency $=T_0$Needs FFT and log: more computation
SIFTAutocorrelation of the LP residualDepends on a good LP analysis
Worked Example: From autocorrelation lag to $T_0$ and $F_0$
Sampling rate $f_s = 8000$ Hz. The largest peak of $R(k)$ after lag 0 is at $k = 80$ samples.
$T_0 = \dfrac{k}{f_s} = \dfrac{80}{8000} = 0.01$ s $= 10$ ms; $F_0 = \dfrac{1}{T_0} = 100$ Hz (adult male range)
Lag 80 at 8 kHz gives $T_0 = 10$ ms and $F_0 = 100$ Hz
Mnemonic Methods ACSW: Autocorrelation, Cepstrum, SIFT, Wavelet. Procedure PFDP: Prepare, Frame + window, Detect, Polish (post-process).
If it comes as 4 marks: give the definition, draw Fig 1.7, and the autocorrelation formula plus the $T_0=k/f_s$ computation – skip the methods table and AMDF.
Quick recall Pitch lag $P_0$ = major non-zero peak of $R(k)$; $T_0 = P_0/f_s$, $F_0 = 1/T_0$. Methods: autocorrelation, AMDF, cepstrum, SIFT, wavelet.

Q6. Define poles and zeros, compare all-pole and all-zero filters, and explain discrete-time convolution with a worked example.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the pole/zero definitions with stability, the FIR/IIR block diagram, and the convolution formula worked step-by-step.

Definition: For $H(z) = B(z)/A(z)$, zeros are values of $z$ making $B(z)=0$ (so $H=0$); poles are values making $A(z)=0$ (so $H \to \infty$). An all-pole filter (IIR) is $H(z) = G/A(z)$; an all-zero filter (FIR) is $H(z) = B(z)$. Convolution combines input $x[n]$ with impulse response $h[n]$: $y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$.
  • All-pole filter: only poles, uses feedback (IIR), stable only if every pole has $|z| \lt 1$; models vocal tract formants (LPC). Examples: Butterworth, Bessel.
  • All-zero filter: only zeros, no feedback (FIR); all its poles sit at $z=0$, inside the unit circle, so it is always stable; models anti-resonances.
  • Convolution steps: Flip $h[k]$, Shift by $n$, Multiply, Sum (Fig 1.9). Output length $= M+N-1$.
  • Properties: convolution is commutative ($x*h = h*x$); the convolution theorem says convolution in time equals multiplication in frequency, $x[n]*h[n] \Longleftrightarrow X(\omega)H(\omega)$.
  • Applications: LTI filters (output = input convolved with impulse response; speech = excitation convolved with vocal tract response), image blurring/edge detection, CNN layers.

Worked Example: Computation of Discrete Convolution
$x[n] = [1, 2, 3]$ ($N=3$), $h[n] = [2, 1]$ ($M=2$). Output length $= M+N-1 = 4$.
$y[0] = 1(2) = 2$; $y[1] = 1(1)+2(2) = 5$; $y[2] = 2(1)+3(2) = 8$; $y[3] = 3(1) = 3$
Convolved Output $y[n] = [2, 5, 8, 3]$
Mnemonic Pole = Peak, Zero = Zero-dip. FIR = Finite and Forever stable; IIR = Inside the circle, or it Runs away. Convolution steps FSMS: Flip, Shift, Multiply, Sum.
If it comes as 4 marks: give the pole/zero definitions, draw Fig 1.8, and the convolution formula plus its worked example – skip Fig 1.9 and the comparison table.
Quick recall All-pole $H(z)=G/A(z)$ (stable if $|z|\lt1$); all-zero $H(z)=B(z)$ (always stable). Convolution $y[n]=\sum_k x[k]h[n-k]$, length $M+N-1$, commutative.

Q7. Explain Power Spectral Density, the Periodogram, the Autoregressive model, and Autocorrelation estimation.

Unit I · 8 marks · the examiner wants the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, the periodogram/AR formulas, and the biased-vs-unbiased autocorrelation example.

Definition: Power Spectral Density (PSD) represents how signal power is distributed across frequency, defined by the Wiener-Khinchin theorem as the Fourier Transform of the autocorrelation sequence $R(k)$.
  • Periodogram (direct non-parametric PSD estimator): $$\hat{S}(\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j\omega n}\right|^2$$ Drawback: high variance and spectral leakage from finite rectangular windowing; reduced by Hamming windowing.
  • Autoregressive (AR) Model: $$x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] + e[n]$$ It is the mathematical foundation of the all-pole filter and LPC; gives a smoother, parametric spectral estimate than the periodogram.
  • Biased autocorrelation: $\hat{R}_b(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum x[n]x[n+k]$ (guarantees a positive semi-definite spectrum). Unbiased: $\hat{R}_u(k) = \frac{1}{N-k}\sum x[n]x[n+k]$ (a true average over the $N-k$ terms actually summed).
Worked Example: Biased vs Unbiased Autocorrelation Estimation
$x = [3, 1, -2, 4, 2]$ ($N=5$). Sums: $k{=}0$: $34$; $k{=}1$: $1$; $k{=}2$: $-6$.
Biased ($\div N=5$): $\hat{R}_b = [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]$. Unbiased ($\div(N-k)$): $\hat{R}_u = [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]$
Biased $= [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]$; Unbiased $= [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]$
If it comes as 4 marks: state the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, the AR model formula, and draw Fig 1.10 – skip the biased/unbiased numeric.
Quick recall Wiener-Khinchin: PSD is the Fourier Transform of autocorrelation. Periodogram is noisy; the AR model gives a smooth spectral envelope, the basis of LPC.

Q8. Explain Linear Prediction, derive the Yule-Walker normal equations, and solve an order-2 prediction example.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the error-filter diagram, the normal-equation derivation, and the complete $a_1,a_2,E_2$ numeric.

Definition: Linear prediction models the current speech sample as a linear combination of $p$ previous samples: $$\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$$ The prediction error is $$e[n] = x[n] - \hat{x}[n]$$
  • Prediction order $p$: number of past samples used, e.g. $p=3$ gives $\hat{x}[n] = a_1x[n-1]+a_2x[n-2]+a_3x[n-3]$.
  • Good predictor: if $\hat{x}[n] \approx x[n]$ then $e[n] \approx 0$; residual is close to white noise.
  • Why speech is predictable: vocal tract inertia correlates adjacent samples; glottal quasi-periodicity correlates samples one pitch period apart.
  • Derivation: minimise mean squared error $E = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n\left(x[n]-\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_k x[n-k]\right)^2$. Setting $\partial E/\partial a_i = 0$ gives the Yule-Walker normal equations: $$\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,R(i-k) = R(i), \quad i=1,\dots,p$$
  • Matrix form ($p=2$): $$\begin{bmatrix} R(0) & R(1) \\ R(1) & R(0) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R(1) \\ R(2) \end{bmatrix}$$
  • Why useful: compression (code only the small residual), signal modeling (few coefficients represent the signal), spectral estimation, forecasting.
Worked Example: Order-2 Linear Prediction, solving the Normal Equations
Given $R(0)=5.0$, $R(1)=3.0$, $R(2)=1.0$: $$\begin{bmatrix} 5.0 & 3.0 \\ 3.0 & 5.0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 1.0 \end{bmatrix}$$
$\Delta = (5.0)(5.0)-(3.0)(3.0) = 16.0$
$a_1 = \dfrac{3.0(5.0)-1.0(3.0)}{16.0} = \dfrac{12}{16} = 0.75$; $a_2 = \dfrac{5.0(1.0)-3.0(3.0)}{16.0} = \dfrac{-4}{16} = -0.25$
$E_2 = R(0) - a_1R(1) - a_2R(2) = 5.0 - 2.25 + 0.25 = 3.0$
$a_1 = 0.75$, $a_2 = -0.25$, Residual Energy $E_2 = 3.0$
Mnemonic "Linear Prediction = Past se Future ka Andaza". Past samples → Prediction; Actual − Predicted = Error; Minimise MSE → Yule-Walker.
If it comes as 4 marks: give the definition, draw Fig 2.1, and the normal-equation formula with just the final answer $a_1=0.75$, $a_2=-0.25$, $E_2=3.0$ – skip the matrix-solve steps.
Quick recall LP predicts $x[n]$ from $p$ past samples; minimising $E$ gives $\sum a_k R(i-k) = R(i)$; example gives $a_1=0.75$, $a_2=-0.25$, $E_2=3.0$.

Q9. Explain Prediction Gain and Segmental Prediction Gain, and compute the prediction gain for a given speech frame.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the formula, interpretation, and the complete Frame-A step-by-step numeric.

Definition: Prediction Gain ($G_p$) measures LP compression efficiency as the ratio of signal variance to prediction-error variance: $$G_p = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_e^2}\right)\ \text{dB}$$ where $\sigma_x^2$ = power of the original signal and $\sigma_e^2$ = power of the residual $e[n] = x[n]-\hat{x}[n]$.
  • Interpretation: large $G_p$ → predictor removes most redundancy, residual close to white noise, effective model. Small $G_p$ → poor prediction.
  • Voiced vs unvoiced: voiced frames (periodic, strong formants) give higher gain; unvoiced (noise-like) give lower gain.
  • Segmental prediction gain (SPG): average of per-frame dB gains over $M$ frames: $$SPG = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} G_p(m)$$
  • Relation to order: increasing $p$ raises gain, but it saturates after a point; typical order $p\approx10$–$16$ for 8–16 kHz speech.
Worked Example: Frame A, voiced-like, $x_A=[2,4,6,5,3,1,-1,-2]$, $p=1$
$R(0) = \sum x^2[n] = 96$ (sum of squares); $R(1) = \sum x[n]x[n-1] = 81$ (products of adjacent samples)
$a_1 = R(1)/R(0) = 81/96 = 0.844$; signal power $\sigma_x^2 = 96/8 = 12$
Residual $e[n] = x[n]-a_1x[n-1]$ for $n=1,\dots,7$: $2.31, 2.63, -0.06, -1.22, -1.53, -1.84, -1.16$, so $\sum e^2[n] = 20.81$
Residual power (average over the 7 residual samples): $\sigma_e^2 = 20.81/7 = 2.97$
$G_p = 10\log_{10}(12/2.97) = 10\log_{10}(4.04) = 6.06$ dB
Frame A: $a_1=0.844$, $\sigma_x^2=12$, $\sigma_e^2=2.97$, $G_p=6.06$ dB (high gain: predictable)

Frame B (unvoiced-like, noisier samples) gives a much lower $G_p = 0.32$ dB, so the segmental gain over the two frames is $SPG = (6.06+0.32)/2 = 3.19$ dB – more correlation always means a higher gain.

Common mistake: $\sigma_e^2$ is the average squared error, $\sigma_e^2 = 20.81/7 = 2.97$, NOT $12 \times 2.97/8 = 4.455$. That wrong value would give $10\log_{10}(12/4.455) = 4.30$ dB, not $6.06$ dB.
Mnemonic "Original / Error → Gain": original power over error power, in dB.
If it comes as 4 marks: give the formula and interpretation, and Frame A's final numbers ($a_1=0.844$, $\sigma_x^2=12$, $\sigma_e^2=2.97$, $G_p=6.06$ dB) – skip Frame B, SPG, and the common-mistake note.
Quick recall $G_p = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2)$ dB; large = good, small = poor; SPG = average of frame gains; Frame A (voiced) 6.06 dB > Frame B (unvoiced) 0.32 dB.

Q10. Explain the Levinson-Durbin algorithm and reflection coefficients, and solve a step-by-step numerical example.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the recursion formulas, the flowchart, and the complete order-2 numeric matching the direct matrix solve.

Definition: The Levinson-Durbin algorithm is an efficient recursive algorithm that solves the Yule-Walker equations in $O(p^2)$ operations, computing predictor coefficients $a_k$ alongside intermediate reflection coefficients $k_m$, using the Toeplitz (symmetric) structure of the autocorrelation matrix instead of inverting it.
  • Toeplitz matrix: the Yule-Walker matrix is symmetric with one constant value per diagonal; Levinson-Durbin exploits this instead of inverting the matrix.
  • New-from-old rule: new $a_j$ = old $a_j$ $-$ $k_m$ $\times$ old $a_{m-j}$ (the mirror coefficient).
  • Stability condition: the all-pole filter is stable if and only if $|k_m| \lt 1$ for every $m = 1,\dots,p$.
  • Complexity: reduces matrix inversion from $O(p^3)$ to $O(p^2)$; space $O(p)$.
  • By-products: reflection coefficients (also called PARCOR), and the error energy $E_m$ at every order (never increases as order grows).
Worked Example: Levinson-Durbin, $R(0)=5.0$, $R(1)=3.0$, $R(2)=1.0$, order $p=2$
Initialization: $E_0 = R(0) = 5.0$.
Iteration 1 ($m=1$): $k_1 = R(1)/E_0 = 3.0/5.0 = 0.6$ ($|0.6|\lt1$, stable); $a_1^{(1)} = k_1 = 0.6$
$E_1 = (1-k_1^2)E_0 = (1-0.36)\times5.0 = 3.2$
Iteration 2 ($m=2$): $k_2 = \dfrac{R(2)-a_1^{(1)}R(1)}{E_1} = \dfrac{1.0-1.8}{3.2} = -0.25$ ($|-0.25|\lt1$, stable)
$a_2^{(2)} = k_2 = -0.25$; $a_1^{(2)} = a_1^{(1)} - k_2\,a_1^{(1)} = 0.6-(-0.25)(0.6) = 0.75$
$E_2 = (1-k_2^2)E_1 = (1-0.0625)\times3.2 = 3.0$
Reflection coeffs: $k_1=0.6$, $k_2=-0.25$; predictor coeffs: $a_1=0.75$, $a_2=-0.25$ (matches the direct matrix solve of Q8)
Common mistake: sign convention. Some books write $A(z) = 1+\sum a_k z^{-k}$ (opposite sign for $a_k$). These notes use $\hat{x}[n] = +\sum a_k x[n-k]$; do not mix the two conventions in one answer.
Mnemonic "R → E → K → a → E": $R(0..p)$ → initial error $E_0$ → reflection coefficient $k_m$ → coefficients $a_j^{(m)}$ → updated error $E_m$.
If it comes as 4 marks: give the recursion formulas, draw Fig 2.3, and only the order-1 step ($k_1=0.6$, $a_1^{(1)}=0.6$, $E_1=3.2$) – state that order 2 continues the same way.
Quick recall Levinson-Durbin solves normal equations in $O(p^2)$; input $R(0..p)$, output $a_k$, $k_m$, $E_p$; stable iff every $|k_m|\lt1$.

Q11. Compare Short-Term and Long-Term linear prediction models, and explain Moving-Average prediction, contrasting AR and MA.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the cascade diagram, the model formulas, and the AR-vs-MA comparison table.

Definition: Short-term prediction (order $p\approx10$–$16$) models the vocal-tract formants; long-term prediction (LTP) models pitch periodicity using lag $T$. A moving-average (MA) model of order $q$ forms the signal from current and past inputs only: $$x[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{q} b_k\,u[n-k]$$
  • Short-term LP: near samples $x[n-1],\dots,x[n-p]$; all-pole $A(z) = 1-\sum a_k z^{-k}$; removes short-lag (formant) correlation.
  • Long-term LP (LTP): predicts the residual from one pitch period back, $e[n]\approx\beta e[n-T]$; error filter $P(z) = 1-\beta z^{-T}$; removes long-lag (pitch) correlation.
  • MA prediction: uses past prediction errors instead of past samples, $\hat{x}[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{q} b_k e[n-k]$; no feedback so it is FIR, $h[n]=b_n$ for $n=0,\dots,q$, always stable.
  • Why AR is preferred for speech: formants are sharp peaks that a few poles model efficiently with linear Yule-Walker equations; an MA model would need a very high order for the same peaks.
  • Where MA appears: nasal sounds (anti-resonances), ARMA models, FIR pre-emphasis filters.
PropertyAR (short-term LPC)MA (all-zero)
Impulse responseInfinite (IIR), decaysFinite (FIR), $q+1$ terms
StabilityNeeds poles inside unit circleAlways stable
Spectral shapeSharp resonant peaksBroad dips / notches
Worked Example: AR(1) vs MA(1), $a_1=b_1=0.8$
AR(1): $x[n]=0.8x[n-1]+u[n]$, so $h[n]=0.8^n$: $1, 0.8, 0.64,\dots$ never exactly zero (IIR).
MA(1): $x[n]=u[n]+0.8u[n-1]$, so $h[n] = 1, 0.8, 0, 0,\dots$ (only 2 nonzero taps, FIR).
AR(1) is infinite and decaying; MA(1) is finite
Mnemonic AR = Apna past Result (past outputs); MA = past input Mixture (past errors); ARMA = dono. AR = Peaks, MA = Zeros.

Q12. Explain Scalar Quantization: Uniform, Optimum Lloyd-Max, Logarithmic Companding, and Adaptive/Differential quantizers.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the staircase diagram, the SQNR formula, the Lloyd-Max conditions, and the companding/DPCM formulas.

Definition: Scalar quantization maps a continuous sample $x$ to a discrete level $y_k = Q(x)$ using decision boundaries $d_k$. Adaptive quantization adapts the step size $\Delta[n]$ to local signal variance; differential quantization (DPCM) quantizes the difference $e[n] = x[n]-\hat{x}[n]$.
  • Uniform quantizer: equal step $\Delta = (x_{max}-x_{min})/L$; mid-tread (0 is a level) or mid-rise (0 is a boundary). $$SQNR \approx 6.02B + 1.76\ \text{dB}$$ each extra bit adds about 6 dB.
  • Optimum Lloyd-Max quantizer: boundary condition $d_k = (y_k+y_{k+1})/2$; reconstruction condition $y_k$ = centroid of its PDF region – minimises MSE for a known PDF.
  • Logarithmic companding: compresses dynamic range before uniform quantization, expands at the receiver. $\mu$-law ($\mu=255$): $C(x) = \dfrac{\ln(1+\mu|x|)}{\ln(1+\mu)}\text{sgn}(x)$. A-law uses $A=87.6$.
  • Adaptive quantization: Forward (AQF) – step size from the input frame, transmitted as side information; Backward (AQB) – step size estimated from past quantized output, no side information needed.
  • DPCM: quantizes $e[n]=x[n]-\hat{x}[n]$; decoder rebuilds $\tilde{x}[n] = \hat{x}[n]+\hat{e}[n]$, predictor in the feedback loop keeps encoder/decoder synchronised.
  • ADPCM: combines an adaptive step size with an adaptive predictor (e.g. ITU G.726 @ 32 kbps).
Worked Example: Uniform SQNR and Companding
3-bit quantizer ($L=2^3=8$ levels): $SQNR \approx 6.02(3)+1.76 = 19.82$ dB.
$\mu$-law for $x=0.5$, $\mu=255$: $C(0.5) = \dfrac{\ln(128.5)}{\ln(256)} = \dfrac{4.8559}{5.5452} = 0.8757$
$SQNR = 19.82$ dB (3-bit); $C(0.5) = 0.8757$
Mnemonic AQF = step size sent Forward (as side info); AQB = estimated Backward (from past output, no side info).
If it comes as 4 marks: give the definition, draw Fig 2.5, and the SQNR formula with the 3-bit numeric – skip Lloyd-Max, companding and AQF/AQB.
Quick recall Uniform $SQNR\approx6.02B+1.76$ dB; Lloyd-Max: boundaries at midpoints, levels at centroids; $\mu$-law compresses weak signals; AQF sends step size, AQB estimates it; DPCM quantizes the difference signal.

Q13. Explain Vector Quantization, distortion measures, and the LBG algorithm for codebook design.

Unit II · 8 marks · the examiner wants the VQ definition, the distortion formulas, and the LBG flowchart with a small worked numeric.

Definition: Vector quantization (VQ) blocks $k$ consecutive samples into a vector $\mathbf{x}$ and maps it to the closest codevector $\mathbf{y}_i$ in a codebook $C=\{\mathbf{y}_1,\dots,\mathbf{y}_N\}$, transmitting only the index $i$.
  • Distortion measures: Squared Euclidean (MSE) $d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sum(x_i-y_i)^2$; Weighted Euclidean $d = (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^T\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})$; Itakura-Saito (spectral distortion between LPC envelopes).
  • LBG algorithm – 4 steps:
    1. Initialise — start with an initial codebook.
    2. Partition — assign each training vector to its nearest codevector (Voronoi region).
    3. Update — recompute each codevector as the centroid of its assigned vectors.
    4. Check convergence — stop when $(D_{old}-D_{new})/D_{old} \lt \epsilon$, else repeat.
  • Codebook types: Full-Search (lowest distortion, $O(N)$ search); Tree-Structured (TSVQ, fast $O(\log_2 N)$ search); Multistage (MSVQ, low complexity for large codebooks); Split VQ (common for LPC parameter quantization).
Worked Example: Tiny LBG 2D Codebook Centroid Update
4 points: $P_1(1,2)$, $P_2(2,4)$, $P_3(7,8)$, $P_4(9,10)$. Initial codevectors $C_1=(4,5)$, $C_2=(5,7)$.
Partitioning: $P_1,P_2$ closer to $C_1$ → region $R_1$; $P_3,P_4$ closer to $C_2$ → region $R_2$.
Updated centroids: $C_1^* = \left(\dfrac{1+2}{2},\dfrac{2+4}{2}\right) = (1.5,3.0)$; $C_2^* = \left(\dfrac{7+9}{2},\dfrac{8+10}{2}\right) = (8.0,9.0)$
Updated LBG Centroids: $C_1^* = (1.5,3.0)$, $C_2^* = (8.0,9.0)$
If it comes as 4 marks: give the definition, draw Fig 2.10, and the four LBG steps – skip the distortion formulas and the codebook-types list.
Quick recall VQ maps a vector to the nearest codebook entry and sends only its index. LBG: partition (Voronoi) → update (centroid) → check convergence, repeat.

1-mark questions

1. Differentiate voiced and unvoiced speech sounds.
Voiced sounds come from vibrating vocal cords giving periodic impulse excitation (vowels); unvoiced sounds use open vocal cords producing random noise excitation (fricatives /s/).
2. What is the Source-Filter Model of speech production?
Speech = excitation source (periodic impulses or noise) filtered by a linear vocal tract system $H(z)$.
3. State the standard human hearing and telephone speech frequency bands.
Human hearing: 20 Hz to 20 kHz; telephone speech band: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (8 kHz sampling).
4. Classify speech coding techniques into three major categories.
Waveform Coders (PCM/ADPCM), Parametric Coders/Vocoders (LPC-10), and Hybrid Coders (CELP family).
5. What is Mean Opinion Score (MOS)?
A subjective speech-quality rating from 1 (unacceptable) to 5 (excellent); toll-quality calls need MOS ≥ 4.0.
6. Define pitch period and fundamental frequency ($F_0$).
Pitch period $T_0$ is one vocal-fold vibration cycle; $F_0 = 1/T_0$.
7. Differentiate All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) filters.
All-pole $H(z)=G/A(z)$ models vocal tract resonances (formants); all-zero $H(z)=B(z)$ models feedforward zeroes.
8. State the discrete-time convolution formula.
$y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$; combines input with filter impulse response.
9. What is the Wiener-Khinchin theorem?
The PSD of a stationary signal is the Fourier Transform of its autocorrelation sequence.
10. Differentiate biased and unbiased autocorrelation estimators.
Biased divides by frame size $N$ (positive semi-definite PSD); unbiased divides by $N-k$.
11. List the types of speech production models.
Physiological, Acoustic (source-filter), Psycholinguistic, and LPC models.
12. Define poles and zeros. Why is an all-zero (FIR) filter always stable?
Zeros make $H(z)$'s numerator zero; poles make its denominator zero. An FIR filter's poles all sit at $z=0$, inside the unit circle, so it is always stable.
13. Define Linear Prediction in speech processing.
Estimation technique predicting $x[n]$ as a weighted sum of $p$ past samples: $\hat{x}[n]=\sum a_k x[n-k]$.
14. Define Prediction Gain (PG).
Ratio of signal variance to prediction-error variance: $G_p = 10\log_{10}(\sigma_x^2/\sigma_e^2)$ dB.
15. State the computational complexity of the Levinson-Durbin algorithm.
Solves normal equations recursively in $O(p^2)$, compared to $O(p^3)$ for direct matrix inversion.
16. What condition on reflection coefficients ($k_m$) guarantees filter stability?
The all-pole filter is stable if and only if $|k_m| \lt 1$ for all $m = 1,\dots,p$.
17. Differentiate short-term and long-term linear prediction.
Short-term LP ($p\approx10$) models vocal tract formants; long-term LP models pitch periodicity $T$.
18. What is Moving Average (MA) prediction?
Predicting the current sample from past prediction errors rather than past speech samples.
19. State the SQNR formula for a $B$-bit uniform quantizer.
$SQNR \approx 6.02B + 1.76$ dB for a full-scale sinusoidal signal.
20. State the two Lloyd-Max optimum quantization conditions.
(1) Decision boundary is the midpoint of adjacent levels, $d_k=(y_k+y_{k+1})/2$; (2) level $y_k$ is the centroid of its PDF interval.
21. What is Vector Quantization (VQ)?
Mapping a $k$-dimensional vector of samples to the nearest codevector in a codebook.
22. What is the LBG algorithm?
An iterative (Generalized Lloyd) codebook design algorithm alternating Voronoi partitioning and centroid updating.
23. What is a Toeplitz matrix? Why does Levinson-Durbin use it?
A symmetric matrix whose every diagonal holds one constant value; the Yule-Walker matrix is Toeplitz, letting Levinson-Durbin solve it recursively in $O(p^2)$.
24. Differentiate AR and MA models.
AR predicts from past outputs, is all-pole (IIR), models sharp peaks; MA predicts from past inputs/errors, is all-zero (FIR), models dips.
25. Differentiate forward (AQF) and backward (AQB) adaptive quantization.
AQF computes the step size from the input frame and transmits it; AQB estimates it from past quantized output (no side information).
