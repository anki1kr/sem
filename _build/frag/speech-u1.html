1.1 Introduction: Speech Signal Processing & Applications

Speech signal processing teaches a computer to record, understand, compress, clean and even produce the human voice. (machine ko aawaz sunna, samajhna aur bolna sikhana)

Definition: Speech signal processing is the branch of digital signal processing that deals with the analysis, manipulation and synthesis of spoken language, for tasks such as speech recognition, speech synthesis, speaker recognition, speech coding and speech enhancement.

Applications:

  • Voice assistants: recognise spoken commands and reply with synthetic speech (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant).
  • ASR transcription: automatic speech recognition turns recordings into text.
  • Speech coding: compresses voice for efficient transmission in mobile and VoIP calls.
  • TTS accessibility: text-to-speech reads text aloud for people with visual or reading impairments.
  • IVR / customer service: interactive voice response menus and voice bots talk to callers.
  • Speaker recognition: identifies or verifies a person from the voice.
  • Speech enhancement: removes background noise and echo from speech.
Mnemonic VAST ISE (iska use VAST hai): Voice assistants, ASR transcription, Speech coding, TTS accessibility, IVR customer service, Speaker recognition, Enhancement.
Quick recall Speech signal processing = analysis, manipulation and synthesis of speech using DSP. Applications: VAST ISE.

1.2 Speech Production Mechanism, Models & Source-Filter Model

Human speech is produced when air pushed from the lungs is shaped by the vibrating vocal folds and acoustic resonance of the vocal tract. (fephdo ki hawa vocal tract se hokar aawaz banti hai)

Definition: Speech production is the physiological process where airflow generated by the lungs passes through the vocal cords (larynx) to create an acoustic excitation source, which is then filtered by the vocal tract cavity (pharynx, oral, and nasal cavities) to produce spoken sounds.

Speech production: a three-stage process

  • 1. Conceptualization: forming the intention to speak and selecting the message to convey. (kya bolna hai, dimaag mein)
  • 2. Formulation: turning the idea into language: choosing the words (lexicalization) and arranging them into sentences. (kaise bolna hai)
  • 3. Articulation: the physical production of sounds by coordinated movement of the respiratory (lungs), phonatory (vocal cords) and articulatory (tongue, lips, jaw) systems. (asal mein bolna)

Key components of speech production:

ComponentWhere / whatRole in speech
LungsChest; the air reservoirProvide the airflow (energy source) needed for all speech
Vocal cords (vocal folds)Inside the larynxVibrate to produce voiced sounds; stay open for unvoiced sounds
Vocal tractAir-filled cavity above the larynx: pharynx, oral cavity, nasal cavityActs as a filter; its shape sets the resonances (formants)
ArticulatorsTongue, lips, jaw, velum (soft palate)Move to change the vocal tract shape and so produce different sounds
Mnemonic Stages CFA: Conceptualize → Formulate → Articulate. Articulators TLJV (Tiger Likes Juicy Vegetables): Tongue, Lips, Jaw, Velum.
  • Voiced Sounds: Produced when vocal cords vibrate quasi-periodically (e.g., vowels /a/, /e/, /i/). Has a distinct fundamental frequency / pitch period $T_0$.
  • Unvoiced Sounds: Produced when vocal cords remain open and air turbulence creates noise at constriction points (e.g., fricatives /s/, /f/, /sh/). No vocal cord vibration.
  • Plosive Sounds: Produced by complete closure followed by sudden air release (e.g., /p/, /t/, /k/).

Source-Filter Model of Speech Production:

Definition: The Source-Filter Model represents speech $s[n]$ as the output of a linear time-varying vocal tract filter $H(z)$ excited by either a periodic impulse train (for voiced speech) or white noise (for unvoiced speech).

Speech production modeling & types of models:

Definition: A speech production model is a simplified representation of how humans produce speech, used to analyse or synthesise speech. Most models treat speech as the output of a system excited by an impulse train (voiced sounds) or white noise (unvoiced sounds).

1.3 Human Auditory System & Perception

The human ear converts mechanical acoustic waves into frequency-analyzed neural impulses via the cochlea. (kaam kahan se chalu hota hai: ear drum se nerve tak)

Definition: The Human Auditory System processes acoustic sound pressure waves through three main anatomical regions: the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear (cochlea), mapping frequencies along the basilar membrane for neural perception.
  • Human Hearing Frequency Range: Standard range is 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). Most sensitive between 1 kHz and 4 kHz.
  • Telephone Bandwidth: Standard speech band for telephony is 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (sampled at 8 kHz in narrowband PCM).
  • Cochlea & Basilar Membrane: The cochlea acts as a biological frequency spectrum analyzer. High frequencies resonate near the stiff base; low frequencies resonate near the flexible apex.
  • Auditory Masking: Phenomenon where a louder sound renders a softer adjacent frequency sound inaudible (frequential/simultaneous masking or temporal masking). Exploited in perceptual codecs.

Detailed parts of the auditory system (class-notes layout):

PartStructuresJob
1. Outer earPinna (auricle), ear canal (external auditory meatus), eardrum (tympanic membrane)Pinna collects sound and funnels it into the canal; the canal carries it to the eardrum; the thin cone-shaped eardrum vibrates with the sound
2. Middle earOssicles: malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), stapes (stirrup); Eustachian tubeOssicles amplify the vibrations and pass them to the oval window of the inner ear; the Eustachian tube joins the middle ear to the back of the throat and equalises air pressure
3. Inner earCochlea (snail-shaped, fluid-filled) containing the organ of Corti, basilar membrane and hair cells; auditory (cochlear) nerveThe basilar membrane vibrates; hair cells (with hair-like stereocilia) on the organ of Corti turn the vibration into electrical signals; the auditory nerve carries them to the brain
4. Pathway to the brainCochlear nucleus, superior olivary nucleus, lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus, auditory cortexBrainstem stations relay the signals and compare both ears; the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe interprets pitch, loudness and location

How hearing works (5 steps):

  1. Sound waves travel through the ear canal to the eardrum and make it vibrate.
  2. The vibrations pass from the eardrum to the ossicles (malleus → incus → stapes) in the middle ear.
  3. The ossicles send the vibrations into the cochlea, a fluid-filled spiral lined with hair cells.
  4. The hair cells move, convert the motion into electrical signals and send them along the auditory nerve.
  5. The brain receives the signals and translates them into sound; this is where the sense of hearing actually happens.
  • Hearing (auditory processing): being aware of sounds and giving them meaning; it needs the outer ear, middle ear, inner ear and auditory nervous system all working together. (sunna = kaan + dimaag dono)
Common mistake: The eardrum sits at the boundary of the outer and middle ear, so books group it differently: the class notes put it in the outer ear, while Fig 1.3 labels it with the middle ear. Write that it separates the two. Also, between the inferior colliculus and the cortex the signal passes the medial geniculate body (thalamus); the class notes skip this relay.
Mnemonic Ossicles in order MIS: Malleus → Incus → Stapes (hammer, anvil, stirrup). Pathway Can Sachin Lead India's Match Again?: Cochlear nucleus → Superior olivary nucleus → Lateral lemniscus → Inferior colliculus → Medial geniculate body → Auditory cortex.
Quick recall Hearing range = 20 Hz to 20 kHz; Telephone speech band = 300-3400 Hz (8 kHz sampling). Cochlea performs frequency decomposition along the basilar membrane. Pathway: hair cells → auditory nerve → cochlear nucleus → superior olivary nucleus → lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → (thalamus) → auditory cortex in the temporal lobe.

1.4 General Structure of Speech Coders

A speech coder compresses digital speech into a compact bitstream for bandwidth-efficient transmission over channels. (speech ko kam bits me convert karna)

Definition: A speech coder consists of an encoder that analyzes and quantizes input speech parameters into a digital bitstream, and a decoder that synthesizes reconstructed speech from the received bitstream.
  • Speech Analysis: Extracts key features such as pitch period, linear prediction coefficients (LPC), frame energy, and spectral parameters.
  • Quantization & Encoding: Converts continuous parameter values into discrete binary index representations.
  • Speech Synthesis: Uses decoded parameters to reconstruct speech approximations via source-filter synthesis models.

Encoder and decoder stages in detail (class notes):

BlockStageWhat happens
Coder (encoder)1. InputDigital speech, obtained by sampling and quantizing the analog speech waveform
2. AnalysisExtracts parameters such as the spectral envelope, pitch and voicing (voiced/unvoiced) information
3. CodingEncodes the parameters into a compact bitstream for transmission or storage
Decoder1. InputReceives the encoded bitstream from the channel
2. DecodingRecovers the parameters and uses them to drive a synthesis model that rebuilds speech
3. OutputDigital reconstructed speech, converted back to analog for playback
  • Mapping to Fig 1.4: Filter/Sample is the input stage, Quantize is the coding stage; Decode + Synthesis form the decoding stage, Output Speech is the output. (naam alag, kaam same)
  • Waveform coders: represent the waveform itself by quantizing and encoding its samples, e.g. PCM, ADPCM, sub-band coding (SBC).
  • Parametric coders (vocoders): model the speech production process with parameters such as vocal tract shape and excitation, e.g. LPC; LPC used with codebook excitation gives CELP.
Mnemonic BQCD (Bhai Quality Chahiye, Delay nahi): Bit rate, Quality, Complexity, Delay.
Quick recall Speech Coder = Encoder (Analysis + Quantization) + Channel + Decoder (Synthesis). Class-notes stages: Encoder = Input → Analysis → Coding; Decoder = Input → Decoding → Output. Considerations: BQCD.

1.5 Classification of Speech Coding Techniques

Speech codecs balance bit-rate efficiency against synthetic speech naturalness across three distinct architectural families. (waveform, parametric aur hybrid me farak)

Definition: Speech coding techniques are classified based on operational mechanisms into: (1) Waveform Coders, (2) Parametric Coders (Vocoders), and (3) Hybrid Coders.
Codec FamilyPrimary MechanismBit-rate RangeQuality (MOS)Standard Examples
Waveform CodersPreserves exact time-domain signal shape sample-by-sample.16 kbps – 64 kbpsHigh (4.0 – 4.5)PCM (G.711 @ 64 kbps), ADPCM (G.726 @ 32 kbps)
Parametric CodersExtracts speech model parameters (pitch, gain, LPC); no shape match.1.2 kbps – 4.8 kbpsLow / Synthetic (2.5 – 3.2)LPC-10 (2.4 kbps), MELP
Hybrid CodersCombines LPC synthesis filter with analysis-by-synthesis codebook excitation.4.8 kbps – 16 kbpsToll / High (3.8 – 4.2)CELP (G.729 @ 8 kbps), AMR, AMR-WB
Mnemonic W-P-H: Waveform (high bit-rate, exact shape), Parametric (very low bit-rate, synthetic model), Hybrid (medium bit-rate, high quality).

Each family in detail (class notes):

  • 1. Waveform coding: tries to reproduce the original waveform sample by sample. It treats all input (speech and background noise) alike, so it is robust to different speakers and noise; it is simple with low processing delay, but needs high bit rates. (shape copy karo, bits zyada)
    • PCM (Pulse Code Modulation): sample, quantize and encode every sample; 64 kbps for telephone speech.
    • ADPCM (Adaptive Differential PCM): codes the difference from a prediction and adapts the quantizer step size to the signal, lowering the bit rate (e.g. 32 kbps).
    • SBC (Sub-band coding): splits speech into frequency bands and codes each band separately, giving more bits to the important bands.
  • 2. Vocoders (source coding): use a model of speech production and perception; they extract vocal tract and excitation parameters and rebuild speech from them at the receiver. Very low bit rates (e.g. 1.2 kbps, 2.4 kbps), but the speech is less natural and the speaker is harder to recognise. (sirf parameters bhejo, aawaz robotic)
    • LPC (Linear Predictive Coding): models each sample as a linear combination of past samples and sends the model parameters.
    • Formant coding: sends the resonances (formant frequencies) of the vocal tract that describe the sound.
  • 3. Hybrid coding: combines waveform matching with a vocoder model, giving better quality than vocoders at lower bit rates than waveform coders. (dono ka best)
    • CELP (Code-Excited Linear Prediction): an LPC filter excited by signals chosen from a codebook.
    • AMR (Adaptive Multi-Rate): switches coding mode and bit rate to suit network conditions and the speech (its modes use ACELP, a CELP type).
    • MBE (Multi-Band Excitation): splits the frequency band into voiced and unvoiced regions, handling speech and background noise flexibly.

1.6 Requirements of Speech Codecs

Designing an optimal speech codec involves balancing four competing parameters: bit rate, speech quality, delay, and computational complexity. (chaar cheezon ke beech balance)

Definition: The performance of a speech codec is governed by four core requirements: low bit rate, high perceptual speech quality, minimal processing delay, and high noise/channel error robustness.

Mean Opinion Score (MOS) Scale for Speech Quality:

MOS ScoreQuality RatingUser PerceptionCodec Benchmark Example
5ExcellentImperceptible distortion, crystal clearUncompressed Audio
4Good (Toll Quality)Just perceptible distortion, acceptable for phone callsPCM G.711 (64 kbps), CELP G.729 (8 kbps)
3FairPerceptible distortion, requires slight effortADPCM G.726 @ lower bitrates
2PoorAnnoying distortion, considerable effort requiredLPC-10 (2.4 kbps)
1UnacceptableExtremely degraded, unintelligibleSevere packet loss / corrupted bitstream
  • Coding Delays:
    • Algorithmic / Frame Delay: Time required to buffer a speech frame (typically 10 ms – 30 ms) plus look-ahead.
    • Processing Delay: Computation time taken by DSP/CPU to run encoder algorithms.
    • Transmission Delay: Time spent transmitting encoded bits over network channels.
    • Why low delay matters: High delay (> 150 ms) creates annoying echo and disrupts natural two-way conversational turn-taking.
  • Robustness: Ability to maintain intelligibility despite channel bit errors, dropped packets (IP networks), and background noise (car/street noise).

1. Quality in detail

  • Speech intelligibility: how easily the words can be understood. (samajh aaya ya nahi)
  • Naturalness: how natural and realistic the decoded voice sounds.
  • Absence of artifacts: no noticeable distortion, added background noise or unnatural sounds introduced by coding.
MetricTypeWhat it measures
MOS (Mean Opinion Score)Subjective (listeners)Listeners rate quality from 1 (unacceptable) to 5 (excellent); the average rating is the MOS
PESQ (Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality)Objective (algorithm)Estimates the quality listeners would perceive after codec and network distortion
SD (Spectral Distortion)Objective, frequency domainDifference between the spectra of the original and the coded speech
SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio)Objective, time domainSignal power compared with coding-error power; does not account for perception
  • SNR formula: $$SNR = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\sum_n x^2[n]}{\sum_n \big(x[n] - \tilde{x}[n]\big)^2}\right)\ \text{dB}$$ where $x[n]$ is the original and $\tilde{x}[n]$ the decoded speech.
Worked Example: SNR of a decoded frame
Original $x = [4, -2, 3, 1]$, decoded $\tilde{x} = [3.9, -2.1, 3.2, 0.9]$
Signal power sum: $4^2 + (-2)^2 + 3^2 + 1^2 = 16 + 4 + 9 + 1 = 30$
Error $x - \tilde{x} = [0.1, 0.1, -0.2, 0.1]$; error power sum: $0.01 + 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.07$
$SNR = 10\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{30}{0.07}\right) = 10\log_{10}(428.57) = 26.32$ dB
SNR is about 26.32 dB (rounded to 2 decimals)

3. Robustness in detail

  • Noise robustness: keeps speech quality in noisy environments (street, car, crowd).
  • Reverberation robustness: keeps speech quality in reverberant (echoing) rooms and halls.
  • Packet loss robustness: handles lost or delayed packets on IP networks without a big drop in quality.
  • VAD (Voice Activity Detection): detects whether a frame contains speech or silence, so silent periods need few or no bits. (chup ho to bits mat bhejo)
  • CNG (Comfort Noise Generation): the receiver plays soft background noise during silence so the line does not sound dead.

1.7 Pitch-Period Estimation

Pitch period represents the fundamental repetition time of quasi-periodic vocal fold vibration during voiced speech. (pitched sound ki repetition cycle count karna)

Definition: Pitch-period estimation (pitch tracking) is the process of determining the fundamental period $T_0$ (or fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$) of a voiced speech segment.
  • Typical Fundamental Frequency ($F_0$) Ranges:
    • Adult Males: 80 Hz to 160 Hz ($T_0 \approx 6.25$ ms to 12.5 ms)
    • Adult Females: 150 Hz to 300 Hz ($T_0 \approx 3.33$ ms to 6.67 ms)
    • Children: 200 Hz to 500 Hz ($T_0 \approx 2.0$ ms to 5.0 ms)
  • Autocorrelation Method: Computes self-similarity of speech signal $x[n]$ across time lag $k$: $$R(k) = \sum_n x[n]\,x[n+k]$$ For voiced speech, $R(k)$ exhibits a prominent secondary peak at lag $k = P_0$, where pitch period $T_0 = P_0 / f_s$.
  • AMDF (Average Magnitude Difference Function): Alternative low-complexity pitch detector that searches for dips/minima: $$\text{AMDF}[k] = \frac{1}{N}\sum |x[n]-x[n+k]|$$
Worked Example: Pitch Period Autocorrelation Estimation
Given synthetic periodic signal sequence ($N = 12$): x = [2, 4, 1, -1, 2, 4, 1, -1, 2, 4, 1, -1]
Compute autocorrelation values $R(k) = \sum_n x[n]\,x[n+k]$ for $k = 0 \dots 5$:
$R(0) = 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2 = 66.0$
$R(1) = 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) = 29.0$
$R(2) = 2(1) + 4(-1) + 1(2) + (-1)(4) + 2(1) + 4(-1) + 1(2) + (-1)(4) + 2(1) + 4(-1) = -10.0$
$R(3) = 2(-1) + 4(2) + 1(4) + (-1)(1) + 2(-1) + 4(2) + 1(4) + (-1)(1) + 2(-1) = 16.0$
$R(4) = 2(2) + 4(4) + 1(1) + (-1)(-1) + 2(2) + 4(4) + 1(1) + (-1)(-1) = 44.0$
$R(5) = 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) + (-1)(2) + 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(-1) = 20.0$
Peak search for $k \gt 0$: Maximum peak occurs at lag $k = 4$ with $R(4) = 44.0$.
Detected Pitch Period $P_0 = 4$ samples ($T_0 = 4 / f_s$)

Why pitch estimation matters: speech analysis, natural-sounding speech synthesis, speaker recognition, speech coding (vocoders send the pitch) and phoneme recognition.

Key methods of pitch-period estimation (class notes):

MethodWorks inHow $T_0$ is foundStrengthWeakness
AutocorrelationTime domainLargest peak of $R(k)$ after the peak at lag 0Simple; works reasonably at low SNRVocal tract resonances (formants) can give larger peaks than the pitch peak
CepstrumQuefrency domain (from the log spectrum)A prominent cepstral peak at quefrency $= T_0$Separates slowly varying vocal tract from fast varying excitation, so fewer formant errorsNeeds FFT and log: more computation
SIFT (Simple Inverse Filtering Technique)Time domain, on the LP residualLP inverse filter removes the vocal tract; autocorrelation of the residual gives the pitch peakClearer, unambiguous pitch peaksDepends on a good LP analysis
Wavelet-basedSeveral scales (like frequency bands)Detects the sharp discontinuities at glottal closures that mark the start of each periodPromising even in noisy speechMore complex to implement
  • Cepstrum: $$c[n] = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\log|X(\omega)|\}$$ the inverse Fourier transform of the log magnitude spectrum. Its index is called quefrency (a time), and the pitch peak appears at quefrency $n = T_0$ (in samples). (log spectrum ka inverse transform)
  • SIFT residual: $$e[n] = x[n] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,x[n-k]$$ then compute $R_e(k) = \sum_n e[n]\,e[n+k]$ and pick its largest peak after lag 0.

General procedure (any algorithm):

  1. Pre-processing: filter out unwanted noise and digitise the analog speech.
  2. Framing and windowing: cut speech into short overlapping frames (about 20-30 ms, about 50% overlap) and window each one: $$x_m[n] = x[n]\,w[n - mL]$$ where $w$ = window (Hamming/Hanning), $m$ = frame index, $L$ = frame shift (hop).
  3. Pitch detection: apply autocorrelation, cepstrum or SIFT to every frame to get $T_0$.
  4. Post-processing (smoothing): filter or smooth the frame-by-frame $T_0$ values to remove errors and get a continuous pitch contour.
Worked Example: From autocorrelation lag to $T_0$ and $F_0$
Sampling rate $f_s = 8000$ Hz. A 20 ms frame holds $0.020 \times 8000 = 160$ samples (hop $L = 80$ for 50% overlap).
The largest peak of $R(k)$ after lag 0 is at $k = 80$ samples.
$T_0 = \dfrac{k}{f_s} = \dfrac{80}{8000} = 0.01$ s $= 10$ ms
$F_0 = \dfrac{1}{T_0} = \dfrac{1}{0.01} = 100$ Hz (inside the adult male range)
If the peak were at $k = 40$: $T_0 = \dfrac{40}{8000} = 5$ ms and $F_0 = 200$ Hz (inside the adult female range)
Lag 80 at 8 kHz gives $T_0 = 10$ ms and $F_0 = 100$ Hz
Mnemonic Methods ACSW (Apna Cricket Score Watch karo): Autocorrelation, Cepstrum, SIFT, Wavelet. Procedure PFDP: Prepare (pre-process), Frame + window, Detect, Polish (post-process).
Common mistake: $R(0)$ is always the largest value (it is the frame energy), so the pitch peak is searched only for $k \gt 0$. Also, the lag is in samples: divide by $f_s$ to get $T_0$ in seconds.
Quick recall Voiced speech is quasi-periodic; pitch lag $P_0$ corresponds to the major non-zero peak of the autocorrelation function $R(k)$. Methods: autocorrelation, cepstrum, SIFT (LP residual + autocorrelation), wavelet; procedure: pre-process → frame + window → detect → smooth.

1.8 All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) Linear Filters

Vocal tract resonances (formants) are modeled as all-pole IIR filters, while anti-resonances (nasals) use all-zero FIR components. (pole = resonance, zero = anti-resonance)

Definition: An all-pole filter (IIR) models feedback resonances with transfer function $H(z) = \frac{G}{A(z)}$, whereas an all-zero filter (FIR) models feedforward zeroes with $H(z) = B(z)$.

Poles and zeros:

Definition: For a transfer function $H(z) = \dfrac{B(z)}{A(z)}$ (a ratio of two polynomials), the zeros are the values of $z$ that make the numerator $B(z) = 0$ (so $H = 0$), and the poles are the values of $z$ that make the denominator $A(z) = 0$ (so $H \to \infty$). Analog filters use $s$ (Laplace domain) in the same way.
  • All-pole filter: constant numerator and polynomial denominator, so it has only poles (no non-trivial zeros). It uses feedback, so it is IIR; it is stable only if every pole lies inside the unit circle, $|z| \lt 1$. (pole = resonance, circle ke andar rakho)
  • All-zero filter: only zeros and no feedback, so it is FIR. All its poles sit at the origin $z = 0$, inside the unit circle, so it is always stable.
  • Examples: Butterworth and Bessel filters are all-pole filters (in their analog prototype form) with no ripple in the passband.
  • All-Zero Filter (FIR / Moving Average): $$y[n] = b_0\,x[n] + b_1\,x[n-1] + \dots + b_q\,x[n-q]$$ Transfer function: $H(z) = B(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{q} b_k z^{-k}$. Always stable.
  • All-Pole Filter (IIR / Autoregressive): $$y[n] = G\,x[n] + \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\,y[n-k]$$ Transfer function: $$H(z) = \frac{G}{A(z)} = \frac{G}{1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k z^{-k}}$$ Represents vocal tract resonances (formants).
Worked Example: All-Pole vs All-Zero Filter Impulse Responses
1. All-Zero FIR Filter: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.5\,x[n-1] - 0.2\,x[n-2]$
Impulse input $x[n] = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]$:
$y[0] = 1.0$, $y[1] = 0.5$, $y[2] = -0.2$, $y[3] = 0.0$, $y[4] = 0.0$.
2. All-Pole IIR Filter: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.6\,y[n-1] - 0.15\,y[n-2]$ ($G = 1$, $a_1 = 0.6$, $a_2 = -0.15$)
Impulse input $x[n] = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]$:
$y[0] = 1.0$
$y[1] = 0.6(1.0) - 0.15(0) = 0.6$
$y[2] = 0.6(0.6) - 0.15(1.0) = 0.36 - 0.15 = 0.21$
$y[3] = 0.6(0.21) - 0.15(0.6) = 0.126 - 0.09 = 0.036$
$y[4] = 0.6(0.036) - 0.15(0.21) = 0.0216 - 0.0315 = -0.0099$
FIR output finite $= [1.0, 0.5, -0.2, 0, 0]$; IIR output infinite decaying $= [1.0, 0.6, 0.21, 0.036, -0.0099]$
Worked Example: Poles, zeros and stability of the example filters
IIR: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.6\,y[n-1] - 0.15\,y[n-2]$ gives $A(z) = 1 - 0.6z^{-1} + 0.15z^{-2}$
Multiply by $z^2$ and set to zero: $z^2 - 0.6z + 0.15 = 0$
$z = \dfrac{0.6 \pm \sqrt{0.36 - 0.6}}{2} = \dfrac{0.6 \pm \sqrt{-0.24}}{2} = 0.3 \pm j\,0.2449$
$|z| = \sqrt{0.3^2 + 0.2449^2} = \sqrt{0.09 + 0.06} = \sqrt{0.15} = 0.3873 \lt 1$, so both poles are inside the unit circle: stable (matches the decaying output above).
FIR: $y[n] = x[n] + 0.5\,x[n-1] - 0.2\,x[n-2]$ gives $B(z) = 1 + 0.5z^{-1} - 0.2z^{-2}$, so $z^2 + 0.5z - 0.2 = 0$
$z = \dfrac{-0.5 \pm \sqrt{0.25 + 0.8}}{2} = \dfrac{-0.5 \pm 1.0247}{2}$, giving zeros $0.2623$ and $-0.7623$; both poles are at $z = 0$: always stable.
Unstable contrast: $y[n] = x[n] + 1.2\,y[n-1]$ has a pole at $z = 1.2 \gt 1$; its impulse response $1, 1.2, 1.44, 1.728, 2.0736, \dots$ keeps growing.
IIR poles $0.3 \pm j0.2449$ with $|z| = 0.3873$ (stable); FIR zeros $0.2623$ and $-0.7623$ (always stable); a pole at $1.2$ is unstable (4 decimals)

Quick recall All-pole filters model vocal tract formants ($H(z) = G/A(z)$); all-zero filters model zeroes ($H(z) = B(z)$). Poles = roots of the denominator (peaks), zeros = roots of the numerator (notches); all-pole IIR is stable only if every pole has $|z| \lt 1$, all-zero FIR is always stable.

1.9 Discrete-Time Convolution

Convolution combines an input signal with a filter's impulse response to compute the filtered output sound. (flip, shift, multiply aur add karo)

Definition: Discrete-time convolution of an input sequence $x[n]$ with an impulse response $h[n]$ produces an output sequence $y[n]$ defined by: $$y[n] = (x * h)[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$$
Worked Example: Computation of Discrete Convolution
Given sequences: $x[n] = [1, 2, 3]$ (length $N = 3$), $h[n] = [2, 1]$ (length $M = 2$).
Output length $= M + N - 1 = 2 + 3 - 1 = 4$ samples ($n = 0 \dots 3$).
$y[0] = x[0]\,h[0] = 1 \times 2 = 2$
$y[1] = x[0]\,h[1] + x[1]\,h[0] = 1(1) + 2(2) = 1 + 4 = 5$
$y[2] = x[1]\,h[1] + x[2]\,h[0] = 2(1) + 3(2) = 2 + 6 = 8$
$y[3] = x[2]\,h[1] = 3 \times 1 = 3$
Convolved Output $y[n] = [2, 5, 8, 3]$

How convolution works (class notes): keep one function fixed, flip the other and slide it across; at every position multiply the overlapping values and add them to get one output value; repeating this for every position builds the new function. (ek ko palto, khiskao, guna karo, jodo)

PropertyStatementMeaning
Commutative$x[n] * h[n] = h[n] * x[n]$The order of the two signals does not change the result
Convolution theorem$x[n] * h[n] \;\Longleftrightarrow\; X(\omega)\,H(\omega)$Convolution in time = multiplication in frequency; with the FFT this can be faster for long signals
LTI output$y[n] = x[n] * h[n]$Output of a linear time-invariant system = input convolved with its impulse response
Worked Example: Commutative check
Swap the order: $y[n] = \sum_k h[k]\,x[n-k]$ with $h = [2, 1]$ and $x = [1, 2, 3]$
$y[0] = 2(1) = 2$, $y[1] = 2(2) + 1(1) = 5$, $y[2] = 2(3) + 1(2) = 8$, $y[3] = 1(3) = 3$
$h * x = [2, 5, 8, 3]$, the same as $x * h$: convolution is commutative
  • Applications: LTI systems and digital filters (output = input convolved with the impulse response; in the source-filter model, speech = excitation convolved with the vocal tract response); also image blurring / edge detection and CNN layers.
Mnemonic Steps FSMS (Fir Se Multiply, Sum): Flip, Shift, Multiply, Sum.
Quick recall Convolution formula $y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$; output length is $M + N - 1$. Convolution is commutative; convolution in time = multiplication in frequency; used in LTI filters, image processing and CNNs.

1.10 Power Spectral Density, Periodogram, Autoregressive Model & Autocorrelation

Spectral analysis transforms time-domain speech samples into frequency domain energy distributions. (frequency domain me energy distribution dekho)

Definition: Power Spectral Density (PSD) represents how signal power is distributed across frequency components, defined via the Wiener-Khinchin theorem as the Fourier Transform of the autocorrelation sequence $R(k)$.
  • Periodogram: Direct nonparametric PSD estimator given by: $$\hat{S}(\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\left|X(\omega)\right|^2 = \frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j\omega n}\right|^2$$ where $\omega = 2\pi f$ (angular frequency) Drawbacks: High variance and spectral leakage due to finite rectangular windowing. Solved using Hamming windowing.
  • Autoregressive (AR) Model: Parametric model where current sample $x[n]$ is expressed as a linear combination of past samples plus an error excitation $e[n]$: $$x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] + e[n]$$ Directly forms the mathematical foundation of All-Pole filter modeling and Linear Predictive Coding (LPC).
  • Autocorrelation Estimation Formulas:
    • Biased Estimator: $$\hat{R}_b(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n]\,x[n+k]$$ (Guarantees positive semi-definite spectrum).
    • Unbiased Estimator: $$\hat{R}_u(k) = \frac{1}{N-k}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x[n]\,x[n+k]$$ (the sum has only $N-k$ terms, so dividing by $N-k$ gives a true average)
Worked Example: Biased vs Unbiased Autocorrelation Estimation
Given short sequence ($N = 5$): x = [3, 1, -2, 4, 2]
Compute $R(0), R(1), R(2)$:
Sum for $k = 0$: $3^2 + 1^2 + (-2)^2 + 4^2 + 2^2 = 9 + 1 + 4 + 16 + 4 = 34$
Sum for $k = 1$: $3(1) + 1(-2) + (-2)(4) + 4(2) = 3 - 2 - 8 + 8 = 1$
Sum for $k = 2$: $3(-2) + 1(4) + (-2)(2) = -6 + 4 - 4 = -6$
Biased ($\div N = 5$): $\hat{R}_b(0) = \dfrac{34}{5} = 6.8$, $\hat{R}_b(1) = \dfrac{1}{5} = 0.2$, $\hat{R}_b(2) = \dfrac{-6}{5} = -1.2$
Unbiased ($\div (N-k)$): $\hat{R}_u(0) = \dfrac{34}{5} = 6.8$, $\hat{R}_u(1) = \dfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$, $\hat{R}_u(2) = \dfrac{-6}{3} = -2.0$
Biased $= [6.8, 0.2, -1.2]$; Unbiased $= [6.8, 0.25, -2.0]$
Quick recall Wiener-Khinchin: PSD is Fourier Transform of autocorrelation. AR model $x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k x[n-k] + e[n]$ provides smooth spectral envelope.

1.11 Short answers (1 mark)

1. Differentiate voiced and unvoiced speech sounds.
Voiced sounds are produced by vibrating vocal cords giving periodic impulse excitation (vowels); unvoiced sounds use open vocal cords producing random noise excitation (fricatives /s/).
2. What is the Source-Filter Model of speech production?
Model representing speech as excitation source (periodic impulses or noise) filtered by a linear system $H(z)$ representing vocal tract acoustic resonance.
3. What is the fundamental role of the cochlea in the auditory system?
The cochlea acts as a biological frequency spectrum analyzer, vibrating along the basilar membrane according to sound frequency.
4. State the standard human hearing and telephone speech frequency bands.
Human hearing: 20 Hz to 20 kHz; Telephone speech band: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz (8 kHz sampling).
5. List the main components of a general speech coder.
Encoder (Filter/Sample → Analysis → Quantize), Bitstream Channel, and Decoder (Decode → Synthesis → Output).
6. Classify speech coding techniques into three major categories.
Waveform Coders (PCM/ADPCM), Parametric Coders / Vocoders (LPC-10), and Hybrid Coders (CELP family).
7. What is Mean Opinion Score (MOS)?
Subjective perceptual speech quality rating scale from 1 (unacceptable) to 5 (excellent). Toll quality phone calls require MOS ≥ 4.0.
8. Why is low coding delay critical in interactive speech communication?
Delays > 150 ms create annoying echo and disrupt natural two-way conversational interactions.
9. Define pitch period and fundamental frequency ($F_0$).
Pitch period $T_0$ is the time interval of one vocal fold vibration cycle; fundamental frequency $F_0 = 1/T_0$.
10. How is pitch period estimated using autocorrelation?
By searching for the major secondary peak location at lag $k = P_0$ in the autocorrelation function $R(k)$.
11. Differentiate All-Pole (IIR) and All-Zero (FIR) filters.
All-pole filters $H(z) = G/A(z)$ model vocal tract resonances (formants); all-zero filters $H(z) = B(z)$ model feedforward zeroes.
12. State the discrete-time convolution formula.
$y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$; combines input $x[n]$ with filter impulse response $h[n]$.
13. What is the Wiener-Khinchin theorem?
States that the Power Spectral Density (PSD) of a stationary signal is the Fourier Transform of its autocorrelation sequence.
14. Differentiate biased and unbiased autocorrelation estimators.
Biased divides by frame size $N$ (guarantees positive semi-definite PSD); unbiased divides by $N-k$.
15. What is speech signal processing? Give four applications.
The branch of DSP that analyses, manipulates and synthesises spoken language. Applications: voice assistants (Siri, Alexa), ASR transcription, speech coding for calls, TTS for accessibility, IVR customer service, speaker recognition.
16. Name the three stages of speech production and the articulators.
Conceptualization (choose the message), formulation (words and sentences), articulation (physical sound production). Articulators: tongue, lips, jaw and velum.
17. List the types of speech production models.
Physiological models, acoustic (source-filter) models, psycholinguistic models and LPC (Linear Predictive Coding) models.
18. Name the ossicles and state the role of the Eustachian tube.
Malleus, incus and stapes carry and amplify eardrum vibrations to the oval window of the inner ear. The Eustachian tube joins the middle ear to the throat and equalises pressure.
19. What do the organ of Corti and the hair cells do?
The organ of Corti inside the cochlea carries the hair cells; their stereocilia turn basilar-membrane vibrations into electrical signals that the auditory nerve sends to the brain.
20. Where is sound interpreted in the brain? Name the brainstem stations on the way.
In the auditory cortex of the temporal lobe. On the way: cochlear nucleus, superior olivary nucleus, lateral lemniscus and inferior colliculus.
21. State the stages of a speech encoder and decoder.
Encoder: input (digital speech), analysis (spectral envelope, pitch, voicing), coding (compact bitstream). Decoder: input (bitstream), decoding (synthesis model), output (reconstructed speech).
22. Give class-notes examples of waveform coders, vocoders and hybrid coders.
Waveform: PCM (64 kbps), ADPCM, sub-band coding. Vocoders: LPC, formant coding (very low rates such as 1.2 or 2.4 kbps). Hybrid: CELP, AMR, MBE.
23. Expand PESQ, VAD and CNG.
PESQ: Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (objective quality metric). VAD: Voice Activity Detection. CNG: Comfort Noise Generation.
24. State the ITU-T G.114 classification of one-way delay.
0 to 150 ms: acceptable for most users; 150 to 400 ms: acceptable depending on the impact; above 400 ms: unacceptable for general network planning.
25. What is SIFT in pitch estimation?
Simple Inverse Filtering Technique: LP analysis and an inverse filter remove the vocal tract, and autocorrelation of the LP residual gives a clear peak at the pitch period.
26. How does the cepstrum method find pitch?
It computes $c[n] = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\log|X(\omega)|\}$, which separates the vocal tract from the excitation; the pitch shows up as a prominent peak at quefrency $T_0$.
27. Define poles and zeros. Why is an all-zero (FIR) filter always stable?
Zeros make the numerator of $H(z)$ zero; poles make the denominator zero. An FIR filter has all its poles at $z = 0$, inside the unit circle, so it is always stable.
28. State the convolution theorem.
Convolution in the time domain equals multiplication in the frequency domain: $x[n] * h[n] \Longleftrightarrow X(\omega)\,H(\omega)$.
29. A pitch peak is found at lag 80 samples with $f_s = 8$ kHz. Find $T_0$ and $F_0$.
$T_0 = 80/8000 = 0.01$ s $= 10$ ms and $F_0 = 1/T_0 = 100$ Hz.
